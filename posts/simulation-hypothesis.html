The Simulation Hypothesis —
Is It Even a Scientific Question?

Nick Bostrom's trilemma says at least one of three propositions must be true. If the third is true — if we are almost certainly living inside a computer simulation — then almost everything you think you know about reality is wrong.

Umakant Kunal
MSc Computer Science

Nick Bostrom published a paper in 2003 with a deceptively simple argument: at least one of three propositions must be true. If the third is true — if we are probably living in a computer simulation — then almost everything you think you know about reality is wrong. The argument has been taken seriously by physicists, philosophers, and Elon Musk. It is worth examining carefully.

The hypothesis sits at a strange intersection. It is precise enough to generate real mathematical estimates. It borrows the vocabulary of empirical science. And yet, as we will see, it may ultimately belong to a domain science cannot touch. The question of whether we live in a simulation might be one of the most important questions we can ask — and one we are constitutionally incapable of answering.

BOSTROM'S TRILEMMA

The argument is clean. Bostrom identifies three propositions and proves that at least one of them must be true:

  1. Civilisations go extinct first. Virtually all technologically advanced civilisations go extinct, or are permanently set back, before reaching the capability to run detailed simulations of ancestral history.
  2. Civilisations choose not to simulate. Virtually all civilisations that reach that capability are, for some reason, uninterested in running large numbers of such ancestor simulations.
  3. We are almost certainly simulated. We are almost certainly living in a computer simulation run by a more advanced civilisation.

The logic is a numbers game. If (1) is false — if civilisations do survive to reach vast computational power — and (2) is also false — if at least some of them run ancestor simulations — then the number of simulated minds will quickly dwarf the number of biological minds. In a universe where billions of civilisations each run millions of simulations, the overwhelming majority of all conscious beings are simulated. By simple probabilistic reasoning, you are almost certainly one of them.

"If we are living in a simulation, then the cosmos that we are observing is just a tiny piece of the totality of physical existence. The physics in the universe where the computer is situated that is running the simulation may or may not resemble the physics of the world that we observe." — Nick Bostrom, Are You Living in a Computer Simulation? (2003)

The argument does not tell us which proposition is true. It is a trilemma, not a proof. But the structure forces a choice. You must either believe that technological civilisations almost always destroy themselves before reaching maturity, or that they almost universally choose not to simulate, or that you are, right now, running on someone else's hardware.

Many people find (1) and (2) implausible given the apparent drive of intelligence toward greater computational capacity. Which would leave (3). That leap — from "we don't have strong reasons for (1) or (2)" to "therefore (3)" — is where the argument gets philosophically dangerous.

THE COMPUTATIONAL ARGUMENT

For proposition (3) to be coherent, the simulation must be computationally feasible. This is not a trivial condition. A simulation that models every atom in every brain of every person who ever lived would require resources so vast as to be meaningless. But a sufficiently advanced civilisation might not need to simulate every atom — only the parts being observed, using something analogous to lazy evaluation or level-of-detail rendering in modern game engines.

The numbers, when you run them, are striking. A rough estimate: the human brain performs around 1017 synaptic operations per second. Simulating the entire conscious experience of humanity — roughly 100 billion people across 200,000 years of existence — requires somewhere between 1033 and 1036 operations in total. This is an astronomical number by today's standards, but not by the standards of a civilisation with access to planetary-scale computing.

A planet-mass computer operating at the physical limits estimated by Seth Lloyd could perform around 1042 operations per second. At that scale, simulating the entirety of recorded human history — every conscious moment, every dream, every conversation — would take less than a millisecond. A civilisation with that capability could run millions of such simulations simultaneously without strain.

The numbers do not rule it out. If anything, they suggest that the computational costs are not a fundamental barrier — they are merely an engineering challenge. An engineering challenge that a civilisation millions of years more advanced than ours might solve the way we solved long division: by building something that does it automatically.

WHAT WOULD A SIMULATION LOOK LIKE FROM INSIDE

Here is where the argument becomes genuinely eerie. If you were designing a simulation for efficiency, what would you build? You would quantise space — there is no point rendering continuous positions if no observable will ever resolve below a certain scale. You would impose a maximum speed of information propagation, to prevent causal paradoxes and simplify the update step. You would make the fundamental laws discrete at deep levels, because continuous mathematics is harder to simulate than integer arithmetic.

Now look at what physics has actually found. Space appears to be quantised at the Planck length: approximately 1.6 × 10−35 metres, a scale so small that nothing in our universe can probe it. The speed of light is a hard limit — information cannot travel faster, as if the universe enforces a strict constraint on non-local updates. Quantum mechanics is, at its core, discrete: energy comes in packets, spin has only two values, measurement collapses a probability distribution to a specific outcome.

And then there are the constants. The fine-structure constant, the cosmological constant, the mass ratio of the proton to the electron — these numbers are fine-tuned to extraordinary precision. Change any of them slightly and the universe becomes hostile to complexity, to chemistry, to life. This is the anthropic argument: of course we observe a universe compatible with our existence, because in a universe incompatible with our existence, no one is observing anything.

But in a simulation, the fine-tuning has a different explanation. The constants are set by the programmer. The universe is not fine-tuned by accident or by anthropic selection — it is fine-tuned because someone chose those values, perhaps because they wanted to see what interesting structures would emerge. The discreteness is not a fundamental feature of reality — it is a computational constraint. The speed of light is not a law of nature — it is a clock rate.

The problem, of course, is that these observations are exactly what we would also expect from a universe that is simply what it is. The features that look like simulation artefacts could equally be features of any physical reality. Anthropic reasoning — the principle that we can only observe conditions compatible with our existence — means we cannot use our own existence as evidence for or against simulation. We are, epistemically, locked inside.

WHAT SCIENCE SAYS

Physicists have, to their credit, taken the hypothesis seriously enough to look for testable predictions. The most famous attempt came from Silas Beane and colleagues at the University of Bonn in 2012. Their paper, Constraints on the Universe as a Numerical Simulation, proposed a specific test: if spacetime is implemented on a regular lattice — the computational equivalent of a grid of pixels — then high-energy cosmic rays should show statistical anisotropies aligned with the lattice axes.

The logic is elegant: any lattice breaks the rotational symmetry of continuous space. The highest-energy particles in the universe, the cosmic rays with energies above the GZK limit, would be sensitive to this symmetry breaking. If the lattice existed, you might see it in the angular distribution of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays.

No such anisotropies have been found. The universe, at every scale we have probed, looks rotationally symmetric. But this is not a decisive result. Beane et al. themselves acknowledged that a sufficiently advanced civilisation might implement the simulation on something more sophisticated than a simple lattice — a structure that preserves rotational symmetry, or that routes computational resources dynamically, only rendering regions under observation. In that case, the test would simply not apply.

"If you were a character in a computer game, you would also be unable to examine the code that makes you exist. Whether it would even be meaningful to ask 'am I simulated?' is itself a question about the nature of meaning." — Max Tegmark, Our Mathematical Universe (2014)

This points to the fundamental difficulty. Every proposed empirical test of the simulation hypothesis can be neutralised by assuming the simulators are sufficiently competent. A universe that looks indistinguishable from a non-simulated universe is, by construction, indistinguishable from a non-simulated universe. We have built our own falsifiability trap.

THE UNFALSIFIABILITY PROBLEM

Karl Popper's criterion for demarcating science from non-science is falsifiability. A hypothesis is scientific if, and only if, there exists some possible observation that would show it to be false. General relativity predicts gravitational lensing — if light did not bend around massive objects, general relativity would be wrong. Evolution predicts a nested hierarchy of species — if we found a mammal in Precambrian rock, evolution would be in trouble. The predictions are specific and risky.

The simulation hypothesis makes no such risky predictions. Whatever we observe, it is consistent with simulation. The speed of light could be a computational limit — or it could just be the speed of light. Quantum discreteness could be a programming artefact — or it could be the fundamental nature of matter. The fine-tuned constants could be set by a programmer — or they could be anthropically selected from a multiverse. Every piece of evidence that seems to support simulation also has a perfectly good non-simulation explanation.

A theory that is compatible with all possible evidence is not a strong theory — it is an unfalsifiable one. The simulation hypothesis, as currently formulated, cannot be falsified from inside the simulation. The simulators, by definition, have the power to make any observation come out any way they choose. This is not a temporary technical limitation. It is a structural feature of the hypothesis itself.

This does not mean the hypothesis is wrong. Unfalsifiable does not mean false. It means the hypothesis has left the domain of empirical science and entered the domain of metaphysics — questions about the nature of reality that cannot, even in principle, be settled by observation. This is not an insult. Metaphysics is a serious discipline. But it is important to be clear about which game we are playing.

The simulation hypothesis, properly understood, is a philosophical claim about the nature of existence. It is not a physics claim. Physicists who endorse it are, whether they know it or not, doing philosophy. That is fine. It is only a problem when the philosophical status of the claim is obscured by the scientific-sounding vocabulary in which it is often dressed.

PHILOSOPHICAL OBJECTIONS

The simulation hypothesis has a distinguished ancestor. In 1641, René Descartes asked whether he could be certain of anything at all. His method of radical doubt led him to a disturbing possibility: what if an evil demon — a malin génie — was deceiving him about everything, feeding his mind a coherent illusion of a physical world that did not exist?

"I will suppose therefore that not God, who is supremely good and the source of truth, but rather some malicious demon of the utmost power and cunning has employed all his energies in order to deceive me." — René Descartes, Meditations on First Philosophy (1641)

Bostrom's simulators are Descartes' demon wearing a lab coat. The structure of the problem is identical: a powerful agent creates a false reality indistinguishable from the true one, and the victim has no internal means of detection. Descartes escaped by noting that the act of doubting proved the existence of a doubter — cogito ergo sum. But this gets you no further than confirming your own consciousness. It says nothing about whether the world you perceive is real.

There is a deeper objection, connected to Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles: if two things are identical in every respect, they are the same thing. If a simulation is indistinguishable from base reality in every possible way — if no experiment, no matter how sophisticated, can tell them apart — then in what sense are they different? The question of whether we are really in base reality starts to dissolve. "Real" becomes a word without referent.

Substrate independence — the idea that what matters about consciousness is the pattern of information processing, not the physical substrate implementing it — has a similar implication. If a mind running on neurons and a mind running on silicon are equally "real" when they have the same functional organisation, then a simulated mind is no less real than a biological one. Which suggests that even if we are simulated, we are genuinely conscious — genuinely experiencing — and the simulation is, in every relevant sense, our reality.

This is the simulation hypothesis converging on solipsism: not "I alone exist" but "the question of what exists outside my experience is meaningless." It is a philosophically respectable position. It is also deeply unsatisfying to anyone who wanted the hypothesis to tell them something about the world.

THE FERMI PARADOX CONNECTION

One of the more intriguing side-effects of the simulation hypothesis is the way it interacts with the Fermi paradox. Enrico Fermi's famous question — if intelligent life is common in the universe, where is everybody? — has no universally satisfying answer. The simulation hypothesis offers one: the apparent emptiness of the universe is a feature, not a bug.

If we are simulated, then the "parent" universe might contain very few advanced civilisations — perhaps only one. That single civilisation runs a vast number of simulations, each populated with minds that experience what appears to be a universe teeming with possibility but mostly empty of other intelligences. The Fermi paradox is solved not because the universe is genuinely empty, but because we are inside a specific simulation designed to look that way — or simply constrained to a small rendered region.

But there is an equally compelling argument running in the opposite direction. Why would a simulator create an empty universe? If you are going to run an ancestor simulation, you would presumably populate it with interesting structures — civilisations, interactions, complexity. A universe that is almost entirely void, in which intelligent life has emerged in one place and has not visibly emerged anywhere else observable, seems like a peculiarly sparse simulation. Perhaps the emptiness is evidence that we are in base reality, not a simulation — because a simulator with unlimited resources would have no incentive to create so much nothing.

The circularity is instructive. The same hypothesis generates contradictory predictions depending on assumptions about the motives and resources of hypothetical simulators. This is not how useful scientific hypotheses behave. It suggests that the simulation framework, however conceptually rich, is not generating falsifiable predictions so much as providing an infinitely flexible interpretive lens.

LIVING WITH UNDECIDABLE QUESTIONS

I have spent time with this question and I do not know the answer. Neither does anyone else. What I have come to believe is that the uncertainty itself is significant — not as a failure, but as a feature of the kind of beings we are.

We are, as far as we know, the only entities in the observable universe that ask questions about the nature of their own existence. We build instruments to probe space and time. We develop mathematics to describe structures we cannot directly observe. We write philosophical thought experiments about evil demons and base realities. And then we hit the wall: the question of whether the universe we experience is the only universe, or a running process on someone else's hardware, may simply be beyond the reach of any method we possess.

That is not a reason to stop asking. Gödel showed us that any sufficiently powerful formal system contains true statements it cannot prove. Turing showed us that the halting problem is undecidable — no algorithm can, in general, determine whether another algorithm terminates. Perhaps some questions about the nature of reality are undecidable in the same structural sense: not because we lack data, but because the question is posed from a vantage point that cannot, by construction, access its own answer.

I find this more interesting than frightening. If we are simulated, the simulation contains minds capable of deducing that they might be simulated, of proving that the question is unanswerable, and of finding that situation philosophically remarkable. If we are not simulated, we are biological machines that arose from chemistry and somehow became capable of asking whether they arose from chemistry — and finding that situation philosophically remarkable. Either way, something extraordinary is happening, and we are the part of the universe where it is happening.

Whether the substrate is carbon or code, I think we can agree: the wondering is real.
