FROM SAND
TO
INTELLIGENCE

A grain of beach sand. A lump of copper ore. A barrel of crude oil. These are the ingredients of a thinking machine. This is the full journey — through physics, chemistry, history, and philosophy — of how the Earth learns to think.

Umakant Kunal
MSc Computer Science

Pick up a handful of beach sand. Feel the weight of it — the coarse grains scraping your palm, warm from the sun. It is inert. It is ancient. Some of those grains were part of a mountain range that no longer exists. They have been tumbling through rivers and oceans for millions of years, ground smaller and smaller by forces that did not care about them at all.

Now look at a modern processor — a chip the size of your thumbnail, etched with 50 billion transistors so small that a strand of human DNA is 25 times wider. It runs at 3 billion cycles per second. It can model climate systems, generate poetry, defeat chess grandmasters, and recognise your face. It is made, in essential part, from that same sand.

The question of how we got from one to the other is one of the most extraordinary stories in human history — a story that spans 4,000 years of metallurgy, 200 years of chemistry, 100 years of quantum physics, 80 years of electronics, and 30 years of software. It is not one story. It is dozens of stories that were each proceeding independently — in different countries, different centuries, different disciplines — until they collided into the single most complex manufactured object humanity has ever produced.

I have been thinking about this for a while. What follows is my attempt to trace the full arc — not just the engineering, but the physics underneath it, the chemistry that makes it possible, the philosophy it provokes, and the strange convergence of timelines that had to happen for any of this to exist at all.

ACT I — THE MATERIALS: WHAT THE EARTH GIVES US

Silicon: The Star of the Show

Silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth's crust, making up about 28% of it by mass. It almost never exists in pure form in nature — it bonds eagerly with oxygen to form silicon dioxide (SiO₂), which is the primary component of quartz, and of common sand. The sand you find on most beaches is largely ground-up quartz — billions of years of geological erosion reducing ancient rock to fine grains of SiO₂.

What makes silicon special is its position in the periodic table. It sits directly below carbon in Group 14, which means it has four valence electrons — the outermost electrons available for bonding. This gives it a crystalline structure of extraordinary regularity when purified, and more importantly, it makes it a semiconductor: a material whose electrical conductivity sits precisely between a conductor and an insulator. That middle ground is the key to everything.

// silicon dioxide → semiconductor grade silicon
SiO₂ + 2C → Si + 2CO carbothermic reduction in electric arc furnace (~1700°C)
Si (metallurgical grade) ~98% pure — not nearly good enough
Si + 3HCl → SiHCl₃ + H₂ convert to trichlorosilane gas for purification
SiHCl₃ → Si (semiconductor) chemical vapour deposition, Siemens process → 99.9999999% pure

That nine-nines purity is not a trivial achievement. Semiconductor-grade silicon is the purest large-scale manufactured substance on Earth. A single atom of phosphorus or boron impurity per billion silicon atoms changes its electrical behaviour significantly. To put that in perspective: if silicon were the population of India, you could have at most one foreign person in the entire country and it would still count as "impure."

Copper: The Veins of the Machine

Every processor, every circuit board, every connection in a computer is threaded with copper. Copper has been worked by humans for over 10,000 years — it was the first metal we mastered, giving its name to an entire age of human civilisation. Its appeal is simple: it conducts electricity better than almost any material except silver, it is ductile enough to draw into incredibly fine wires, and it is relatively abundant.

Inside a modern processor, copper wires connect transistors through up to 15 layers of metal interconnects, each wire thinner than a virus. The total length of copper wiring inside a single chip, if you could unravel it, would stretch for kilometres. This copper is deposited atom-by-atom using a process called electrochemical deposition — essentially electroplating at a nanometre scale, a technique whose fundamental chemistry was understood by Michael Faraday in the 1830s.

Rare Earth Elements: The Invisible Ingredients

Less visibly, a modern computer contains traces of neodymium (in speakers and motors), europium and terbium (in displays), indium (in touchscreens as indium tin oxide), and tantalum (in capacitors). These rare earth elements, extracted from mines primarily in China, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and Australia, represent the geological lottery that makes modern electronics possible. Some of them were unknown to science 200 years ago.

Plastics: The Skeleton

The case of your laptop, the insulation on every wire, the circuit board substrate itself — these are polymers, long-chain carbon molecules derived from crude oil. The circuit board is typically FR4, a glass-reinforced epoxy resin that is an excellent electrical insulator, resists heat, and can be drilled and etched with precision. It descends, chemically, from coal-tar chemistry experiments in the mid-19th century — specifically from Leo Baekeland's invention of Bakelite in 1907, the first fully synthetic plastic.

"We are living in the plastic age. No material has so completely transformed our civilisation in so short a time." — J. Harry DuBois, Plastics History USA, 1972

What is philosophically striking about plastics is that they represent something entirely new in nature: materials that did not exist before humans invented them. Every mineral, every metal, every crystal is found somewhere on Earth. Plastics are not. They are a purely synthetic addition to the material world — molecules assembled by human intelligence into configurations that nature never produced. In a small way, every plastic component in a computer is already evidence of intelligence transforming matter.

ACT II — THE PHYSICS: WHY ANY OF THIS WORKS

Quantum Mechanics Changes Everything

To understand why silicon is a semiconductor, you need quantum mechanics. Not as a metaphor — literally, the quantum mechanical behaviour of electrons in crystal lattices is what makes transistors possible. Without quantum mechanics, there are no semiconductors. Without semiconductors, there is no computing. The entire digital world floats on a foundation of quantum physics that was considered impossibly abstract when it was first developed.

When atoms bond together in a crystalline solid, their individual electron energy levels merge into broad energy bands. The two critical bands are the valence band (where electrons are bound to atoms) and the conduction band (where electrons can move freely and carry current). Between them is a gap — the band gap.

In a conductor (copper, gold) — the bands overlap, electrons flow freely. In an insulator (glass, rubber) — the band gap is large (~9 eV for diamond), electrons cannot jump it. In a semiconductor (silicon) — the band gap is small (1.1 eV for silicon). At room temperature, thermal energy is just enough to push some electrons across. Add a tiny electric field, shine light on it, or introduce specific impurity atoms — and you can control exactly how many electrons cross. That controllability is the transistor.

Doping: Writing Information into Matter

Pure silicon is not very interesting electrically. The magic begins when you introduce impurities — a process called doping. Add phosphorus atoms (Group 15, five valence electrons) and you get n-type silicon — extra electrons available for conduction. Add boron atoms (Group 13, three valence electrons) and you get p-type silicon — "holes" where electrons are missing, which behave like positive charge carriers.

When you place n-type and p-type silicon in contact, you create a p-n junction — a diode. Current flows easily one way, but not the other. Place two p-n junctions back to back with a thin layer in between, and you have a transistor: a device where a tiny voltage on the middle layer (the gate) controls a much larger current flowing through the outer layers. This is amplification. This is switching. This is, at its most fundamental level, the 1 and the 0 of digital computing.

"The transistor was perhaps the most important invention of the twentieth century, and the story of its invention is a human drama of collaboration, rivalry, and serendipity." — Michael Riordan and Lillian Hoddeson, Crystal Fire, 1997

Quantum Tunnelling: The Ghost in the Machine

As transistors have shrunk to the scale of a few atoms, something unsettling has emerged: quantum tunnelling. An electron, described quantum mechanically as a probability wave rather than a definite particle, has a non-zero probability of passing through a barrier it classically should not be able to cross. At 2-nanometre transistor scales — the current frontier — electrons routinely tunnel through the gate oxide, leaking current even when the transistor is "off." This is one of the fundamental physical limits approaching us in semiconductor manufacturing, and it is a limit written into the laws of quantum mechanics, not merely an engineering problem.

There is something philosophically rich here: the very quantum effects that enable the transistor at one scale begin to destroy it at a smaller scale. The physics gives with one hand and takes with the other.

ACT III — THE TIMELINE: HOW THE THREADS CONVERGED

The computer did not emerge from a single lineage of invention. It emerged from the collision of at least seven independent research traditions, each proceeding for decades with no idea the others existed, until in the mid-20th century they crashed into each other with extraordinary force.

~3000 BCE — 1000 CE
Metallurgy and materials. Humans master copper, bronze, iron, and steel. The craft knowledge of working metals — smelting, alloying, annealing — accumulates over millennia. This tradition will eventually produce the ultra-pure silicon and precision copper interconnects of the microchip, though no one working a Bronze Age forge could have imagined it.
1600s — 1800s
Chemistry becomes a science. Lavoisier establishes conservation of mass (1789). Dalton proposes atomic theory (1803). Mendeleev publishes the periodic table (1869). For the first time, humanity has a systematic map of matter. The concept of an "element" — a substance that cannot be chemically decomposed — is formalised. Silicon is isolated by Jöns Jacob Berzelius in 1823, named, and classified. No one yet has any idea what it will be used for.
1820s — 1870s
Electromagnetism unified. Faraday discovers electromagnetic induction (1831) — the conversion of motion to electricity and back. Maxwell writes his equations (1865), showing electricity and magnetism are aspects of a single force, and predicting radio waves. This thread will eventually produce every power supply, motor, transformer, and radio in the modern world — and explain why copper conducts.
1847 — 1910s
Boolean logic and formal mathematics. George Boole publishes "The Laws of Thought" (1854) — reducing logical reasoning to algebraic operations on 0s and 1s. Frege, Russell, and Whitehead spend decades trying to reduce all of mathematics to formal logic. This tradition seems entirely abstract — pure mathematics with no physical application. Until Claude Shannon in 1937 writes his Master's thesis showing that Boolean algebra can describe electrical circuits.
1900 — 1935
Quantum mechanics developed. Planck proposes energy quantisation (1900). Einstein explains the photoelectric effect (1905). Bohr models the hydrogen atom (1913). Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Dirac, and Pauli develop full quantum mechanics (1925–1928). Band theory of solids (Bloch, 1928) — the theoretical foundation for understanding semiconductors — is published. No one working on these problems thinks about computers. They are trying to understand the atom.
1936 — 1945
The concept of computation born. Alan Turing publishes "On Computable Numbers" (1936), inventing the theoretical Turing machine — a mathematical model of what it means to compute. Von Neumann independently develops the stored-program architecture. The Second World War accelerates everything: Colossus (1943), ENIAC (1945) — the first large-scale electronic computers — are built using vacuum tubes. They fill entire rooms. They fail constantly. They are just barely possible.
1947
The transistor. Shockley, Bardeen, and Brattain at Bell Labs invent the transistor on December 23, 1947. This is the moment all six previous timelines collide. The transistor is: a materials science achievement (purified germanium), a quantum mechanics achievement (p-n junction physics), a chemistry achievement (doping), an electrical engineering achievement (circuit integration), and the physical embodiment of a Boolean logic gate. Six separate centuries of human knowledge compressed into a device the size of a paperclip.
1958 — 1971
The integrated circuit and the microprocessor. Jack Kilby (Texas Instruments) and Robert Noyce (Fairchild) independently invent the integrated circuit in 1958–1959 — multiple transistors on a single piece of semiconductor. Intel's 4004 (1971) puts 2,300 transistors on a chip 4mm × 3mm — a complete processor. The race begins. Moore's Law — Gordon Moore's 1965 observation that transistor density doubles roughly every two years — becomes both description and prophecy.
1970s — 1990s
Software, operating systems, internet. Unix (1969), C language (1972), TCP/IP (1974), personal computers (1977), the World Wide Web (1989). The physical machine acquires a mind — layers of abstraction piled on top of transistors, until the raw silicon is completely invisible to the person using it. The separation of hardware and software becomes the defining architecture of the information age.
1986 — 2012
Machine learning and neural networks. Backpropagation formalised by Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams (1986). Support vector machines (1995). The first GPU-accelerated neural network training. AlexNet wins ImageNet (2012) by a margin that ends a decade of scepticism about deep learning. The same physical transistors that once ran word processors now simulate, very roughly, the behaviour of neurons.
2017 — present
The transformer and large language models. "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017) introduces the transformer architecture. GPT-3 (2020), GPT-4 (2023), Claude, Gemini. Billions of transistors, switched trillions of times per second, performing floating-point arithmetic on matrices of numbers that represent, in some abstract sense, the patterns of human language and thought. The sand has come a long way.

ACT IV — THE MANUFACTURING: MAGIC DISGUISED AS ENGINEERING

Growing a Crystal

After silicon is purified to nine-nines purity, it is melted at 1,414°C and a seed crystal is slowly rotated and withdrawn from the melt — the Czochralski process, invented by Polish chemist Jan Czochralski in 1916, accidentally, when he dipped his pen into a crucible of molten tin instead of his inkwell and pulled out a single crystal filament. The result is an ingot of single-crystal silicon up to 300mm in diameter and 2 metres long — a perfect atomic lattice with almost no defects, more structurally perfect than any natural crystal on Earth.

This ingot is sliced into wafers 0.7mm thick using diamond wire saws. Each wafer will eventually hold hundreds of chips. The surface is polished to an atomic-level flatness — variations of less than a single atom across the entire diameter. A human hair placed across the wafer would be a geographical feature.

Photolithography: Printing with Light

The transistors are not carved into silicon — they are printed onto it, using light, in a process called photolithography. A layer of photosensitive material (photoresist) is spin-coated onto the wafer. A mask containing the circuit pattern is projected onto it using UV light, which chemically changes the resist where it falls. The exposed (or unexposed) resist is washed away, leaving a precise pattern. Chemicals etch the exposed silicon. More materials are deposited. The process repeats — up to 100 times for a modern chip.

The challenge is the wavelength of light. The finest features you can print are limited by the wavelength of the light you use — diffraction prevents you from printing features smaller than roughly half the wavelength. Today's leading chipmakers use Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV) lithography — light with a wavelength of 13.5 nanometres, generated by firing a 50,000-pulse-per-second laser at droplets of molten tin, which vaporises them into a plasma that emits EUV radiation. The entire optical path must be in a vacuum, because EUV is absorbed by air. The mirrors guiding this light are the most reflective objects ever manufactured by humans.

A modern 3nm transistor gate is approximately 15 silicon atoms wide. The chip in your phone contains roughly 15 billion transistors in an area smaller than your fingernail. If each transistor were the size of a person, the chip would cover the land area of India — and there would be 15 billion people on it. The entire manufacturing process that produces this must maintain cleanliness levels millions of times purer than a hospital operating theatre, because a single dust particle anywhere in the process destroys the chip.

The Fab: A Temple of Controlled Environment

A semiconductor fabrication plant — a "fab" — is perhaps the most complex manufacturing environment ever created. TSMC's most advanced fab in Taiwan cost $20 billion to build. Workers wear full "bunny suits" — complete body coverings that prevent any human skin or hair from contaminating the environment. The air is filtered to Class 1 cleanroom standards: fewer than one particle larger than 0.1 micrometres per cubic foot. The building sits on active vibration-damping systems, because a truck driving past on a road half a kilometre away creates vibrations large enough to ruin a wafer. The temperature is controlled to within 0.01°C.

And all of this — the extreme purity, the vibration isolation, the laser-vaporised tin plasma, the atomic polishing — is performed continuously, at industrial scale, producing hundreds of millions of chips per year, each one sold for tens to hundreds of dollars. The economics of this are as extraordinary as the physics.

ACT V — THE PHILOSOPHY: WHAT DOES IT MEAN?

Matter Becoming Mind

There is a version of the story I have told above that is purely mechanical — chemistry equations and process steps and engineering milestones. But there is another version that I find more interesting, and more unsettling. It is the version where you step back and ask: what actually happened here?

A configuration of silicon atoms — arranged not by geological forces but by human intention — performs operations that we call "thinking." It recognises patterns in images. It translates between languages it was never explicitly taught the grammar of. It writes code, composes music, models proteins. Whatever we decide to call this, it is something new in the universe: matter organised by intelligence, organising itself to exhibit something that resembles intelligence.

"Man is nothing else but what he makes of himself." — Jean-Paul Sartre, Existentialism Is a Humanism, 1946

Sartre was talking about human beings. But the statement has an eerie resonance when applied to the machines we are building. A computer, in some sense, is nothing else but what we made of it — matter given form and function entirely by human purpose. And what we are making of it, increasingly, is something that reasons. The philosophical question is whether there is a difference in kind between matter organised to think and a mind that arose from matter through evolution — or whether the difference is merely one of degree.

The Convergence Problem: No One Planned This

One of the things that strikes me most about this history is how utterly unplanned it was. George Boole was not trying to build a computer. He was trying to reduce thought to algebra, as a philosophical project. Max Planck quantised energy not because he wanted to but because it was the only mathematical trick that fit the experimental data — he found it deeply uncomfortable and spent years trying to find an alternative. The Czochralski process was a laboratory accident. ARPANET — the predecessor to the internet — was built to survive nuclear war, not to deliver cat videos.

What emerged from the collision of these independent, accidentally-converging threads is a technology whose complexity is genuinely beyond any individual human's comprehension. No person alive understands, in complete detail, how a modern processor is designed, manufactured, programmed, and operated. It is a product of distributed cognition across centuries — knowledge accumulated by thousands of people who never met, speaking different languages, working in different centuries, on problems that appeared to have nothing to do with each other.

"If I have seen further, it is by standing on the shoulders of giants." — Isaac Newton, letter to Robert Hooke, 1675

Newton was talking about optics. But the image applies perfectly to the computer. Every chip manufactured today stands on the shoulders of Boole, Faraday, Planck, Turing, Shockley, and ten thousand others. It is, in the most literal sense, crystallised collective intelligence — the accumulated understanding of centuries, frozen into silicon.

The Recursion: Intelligence Building Intelligence

There is one final twist in this story that I cannot stop thinking about. The tools we use to design modern chips are themselves AI systems running on those same chips. TSMC could not manufacture a 3nm chip without AI-powered design verification tools. Those AI tools could not exist without the chips that came before them. We have entered a loop: intelligence is now designing the substrate of intelligence.

The process is not self-contained. It still requires human engineers, human intention, human capital. But the degree of human involvement at each layer is decreasing. AI designs the layout of transistors. AI writes the compiler that converts code to machine instructions. AI trains the next generation of AI systems. The sand does not know what it has become. But perhaps that is the wrong way to think about it.

"The universe seems neither benign nor hostile, merely indifferent to the concerns of such puny creatures as we." — Carl Sagan, Cosmos, 1980

Sagan was making a point about cosmic scale — that the universe does not care about us. But there is a counter-narrative available now: the universe is not entirely indifferent. At least in one small corner of it, matter has organised itself — through four billion years of evolution and ten thousand years of accumulated knowledge — into something that looks back at the universe and asks questions about it. The silicon in your processor was formed in the explosion of a star that died before our sun was born. Quite literally, a star is thinking about itself.

ACT VI — WHERE WE ARE AND WHERE THIS GOES

We are approaching the physical limits of silicon-based computing. At 2nm scale, quantum effects that were once negligible dominate. The roadmap beyond silicon includes: III-V semiconductors (gallium arsenide, indium phosphide) with higher electron mobility; carbon nanotubes whose diameter is 1nm and which exhibit superior electrical properties; two-dimensional materials like graphene and molybdenum disulfide which are literally one atom thick; and eventually, perhaps, quantum computers where the qubits exploit superposition and entanglement rather than fighting them.

Each of these paths requires its own convergence of physics, chemistry, and engineering — its own collision of timelines. Graphene was isolated using Scotch tape and a pencil in 2004 by Geim and Novoselov, who won the Nobel Prize six years later. The material that might power the next generation of computing was discovered with an office supply. The pattern holds: the most important discoveries come from unexpected directions, by people who were not trying to solve the problem you needed solved.

A PERSONAL NOTE

I study artificial intelligence. I write code. I build systems. And yet I regularly feel a kind of vertigo when I think clearly about what I am actually using. Every line of Python I write is executed by a chip whose manufacture required the most precise physical process in human history. Every model I train runs on silicon whose behaviour is governed by equations that took 300 years to develop, equations that describe a reality so strange that their discoverers found them philosophically disturbing.

The distance between a grain of sand on a beach and a transformer model generating language is so vast — in physics, in chemistry, in engineering, in time — that it should feel impossible. The fact that it is not impossible, that humans actually managed this, is something I think we have collectively failed to appreciate. We take it for granted because it already exists. But it did not have to exist. It almost did not exist. It exists because of an extraordinary, accidental, multi-century convergence of human curiosity about things that seemed to have no practical application.

Boole was thinking about thought. Planck was thinking about light. Turing was thinking about what it means to compute. None of them were thinking about you reading this on a screen. And yet here we are.

The next time you hold your phone — warm from running, lighter than a book — remember that you are holding a machine made of ancient mountains and dead stars, shaped by collective human intelligence spanning centuries, running on quantum physics that no one fully understands, executing instructions written in languages that are themselves the product of decades of computer science research. It is, by any honest measure, the most complex object in the known universe relative to its size. And it started as sand.
