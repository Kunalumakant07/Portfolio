Shor's Algorithm —
The Math That Could
Break the Internet

Every HTTPS padlock in your browser rests on a single assumption: that factoring large numbers is hard. Peter Shor proved that assumption expires the moment we build a large enough quantum computer. Here's exactly how — and what happens next.

Umakant Kunal
MSc Computer Science

Right now, thousands of encrypted connections are protecting your banking sessions, your passwords, your private messages. Every single one of them depends on a mathematical fact that has never been proven — only assumed: that multiplying two large prime numbers is easy, but undoing that multiplication is computationally impossible.

In 1994, a mathematician named Peter Shor showed that this assumption is not a law of nature. It is a limitation of classical computers. On a sufficiently large quantum computer, factoring a 2048-bit number — the kind that secures most of the internet today — would take hours, not the age of the universe. The internet's security model has an expiry date. We just don't know exactly when it arrives.

This post is about the mathematics behind that claim: why RSA works, what Shor actually discovered, how the Quantum Fourier Transform makes it possible, and where we actually stand today. No quantum mechanics background required — just curiosity and a tolerance for beautiful ideas.

RSA ENCRYPTION EXPLAINED

RSA — named after its inventors Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman — is the bedrock of public-key cryptography. Understanding why it works requires about three minutes of number theory.

The scheme rests on a simple fact about prime multiplication. Take two primes: p = 61 and q = 53. Their product is n = 3233. I just did that in my head. Now try the reverse: given only 3233, find the two primes that multiply to give it. You'd have to try divisors until you found them. For small numbers that's easy. For a 2048-bit n — a number with 617 decimal digits — it is not.

Here's how RSA uses this asymmetry. You choose n = p × q, then compute a related value φ(n) = (p−1)(q−1) — in our example, 60 × 52 = 3120. You pick a public exponent e (commonly 65537) that shares no common factor with φ(n), then calculate a private exponent d such that e × d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)).

RSA key generation (small example) p = 61, q = 53 n = p × q = 3233 φ(n) = (p−1)(q−1) = 3120 e = 17 (public exponent, gcd(17, 3120) = 1) d = 2753 (private exponent: 17 × 2753 ≡ 1 mod 3120) Public key: (n=3233, e=17)  |  Private key: d=2753

To encrypt a message M: compute C = Me mod n. To decrypt: M = Cd mod n. The magic of modular arithmetic (Euler's theorem, specifically) guarantees these operations are inverses of each other — but only if you know d.

And d is computable only if you know φ(n). And φ(n) is computable only if you know p and q. So the entire security of RSA collapses to a single question: can an attacker factor n?

"The security of RSA relies on the difficulty of factoring large integers. We have not proved that this is actually difficult; we just have no good algorithms for doing it." — Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir & Leonard Adleman, "A Method for Obtaining Digital Signatures and Public-Key Cryptosystems", 1978

Notice the honesty in that phrasing. We just have no good algorithms for doing it. RSA was never proven secure. It was bet secure. For forty-eight years, that bet has held — on classical hardware.

THE QUANTUM SPEEDUP

The best classical algorithm for integer factorization is the General Number Field Sieve (GNFS), discovered in 1990. It is genuinely impressive mathematics. Its runtime, however, is sub-exponential but still brutally large for big inputs:

General Number Field Sieve complexity Time ≈ exp( c · (log N)1/3 · (log log N)2/3 ) where c ≈ 1.923 for factoring

For a 2048-bit RSA key, this works out to roughly 1034 operations — more operations than there are atoms in the Earth. Current supercomputers executing a billion operations per second would need longer than the age of the universe. The largest RSA key ever factored classically was 829 bits (RSA-250), in 2020, using thousands of CPU-years of compute.

Shor's algorithm changes the equation entirely. Its complexity is:

Shor's algorithm complexity Time = O( (log N)3 ) Space = O( log N )

That is polynomial time. For 2048-bit RSA, instead of 1034 operations, Shor's algorithm needs roughly 20483 ≈ 8.6 billion operations. A fast classical computer could execute that many operations in seconds — if it were running a quantum algorithm. The speedup is not incremental. It is categorical: the problem moves from a different class entirely.

To be concrete: breaking RSA-2048 classically with GNFS would take longer than the current age of the universe on the best hardware we have. Breaking it with a large-scale Shor implementation would take — depending on quantum hardware — hours to days.

HOW SHOR'S ALGORITHM WORKS

The genius of Shor's algorithm is a reduction: it converts the factoring problem into a different problem — period-finding — that quantum computers can solve exponentially faster than classical ones.

Step 1: Reduce Factoring to Period-Finding

Pick a random integer a with 1 < a < N. If gcd(a, N) ≠ 1, you got lucky — you already found a factor. Otherwise, consider the function:

The periodic function at the heart of Shor's f(x) = ax mod N

This function is periodic — it repeats itself. The length of its period is called r. By number theory, once you know r, you can recover factors of N with high probability. Specifically, if r is even, then gcd(ar/2 − 1, N) and gcd(ar/2 + 1, N) are likely to be non-trivial factors of N.

The problem is: classically, finding r requires evaluating f(x) for exponentially many values of x. That's the bottleneck. And this is exactly where quantum mechanics steps in.

Step 2: Superposition Over All Inputs

A quantum computer can place a register in a superposition — simultaneously representing every possible value of x from 0 to Q−1 (where Q is chosen as a power of 2 slightly larger than N²). Evaluating the quantum circuit for f on this superposition computes f(x) for all x at once, in a single quantum operation.

After measurement, the output register collapses to some value f(x₀) = ax₀ mod N. But here's the key: the input register does not collapse to x₀. It collapses to the uniform superposition of all x that give the same output — namely x₀, x₀+r, x₀+2r, x₀+3r, ... The period r is now encoded in the quantum state.

Step 3: Extract the Period with the QFT

The input register now holds a periodic state with spacing r. To extract r, Shor applies the Quantum Fourier Transform — the quantum analogue of the discrete Fourier transform. The QFT converts a periodic signal in the time domain to a sharp spike in the frequency domain. After QFT, measuring the register gives a value close to a multiple of Q/r. A few measurements and a classical continued-fractions algorithm extract r exactly.

Step 4: Classical Post-Processing

With r in hand: check that r is even and that ar/2 ≢ −1 (mod N). If so, compute p = gcd(ar/2−1, N) and q = gcd(ar/2+1, N). With high probability, these are the prime factors. If not, repeat with a different random a. Expected repetitions: 1–2.

Shor's algorithm doesn't "try" factors. It restructures the problem: factoring becomes period-finding becomes Fourier analysis — and quantum computers are exponentially good at Fourier analysis on superposed states. The trick is the reduction, not the raw power.

QUANTUM FOURIER TRANSFORM

The QFT is arguably the most important subroutine in all of quantum computing. It does what the classical Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) does — map a function from the time domain to the frequency domain — but in quantum superposition, acting on all amplitudes simultaneously.

The classical DFT on N points takes O(N log N) operations (the Fast Fourier Transform). The QFT on N = 2n points takes only O(n²) = O((log N)²) quantum gates. This is not a constant-factor improvement. It is an exponential compression — the quantum state encodes all N amplitudes in n qubits, and the QFT operates on all of them at once.

QFT unitary (acts on computational basis states) QFTN |j⟩ = (1/√N) · Σk=0N−1 e2πijk/N |k⟩ where N = 2n, j ∈ {0, 1, ..., N−1} and ω = e2πi/N is the N-th root of unity

In matrix form, the QFT is a unitary matrix whose (j,k) entry is ωjk/√N. What makes this remarkable: a classical computer needs O(N) memory to store the vector it acts on. A quantum computer stores the same information in O(log N) qubits — and the QFT circuit operates directly on those qubits with O(n²) Hadamard and controlled-phase gates.

Intuitively: the QFT takes a quantum state that encodes a periodic pattern — like the superposition of x₀, x₀+r, x₀+2r, ... that Shor's algorithm creates — and transforms it into a state concentrated around frequencies that are integer multiples of 1/r. Measuring gives you information about 1/r, from which r follows. The QFT is the lens that makes the hidden period visible.

"The quantum Fourier transform is the key subroutine in almost all known quantum algorithms that provide exponential speedups over classical computation. What's remarkable is that it achieves in polynomial time what would require exponential time classically — not because of parallelism in the naive sense, but because of quantum interference." — Peter Shor, "Polynomial-Time Algorithms for Prime Factorization and Discrete Logarithms on a Quantum Computer", SIAM Journal on Computing, 1997

WHERE WE ACTUALLY ARE

Here is the honest answer: Shor's algorithm works. We know this because it has been run — on very small inputs. In 2001, IBM ran it on a 7-qubit NMR quantum computer and factored 15 = 3 × 5. In 2012, researchers factored 21 = 3 × 7. In subsequent years, various labs have factored numbers up to a few hundred using increasingly clever shortcuts and noise-mitigation techniques.

The gap between 21 and 2048-bit RSA is not a gap of degree. It is a gap of kind.

To factor an n-bit RSA key using Shor's algorithm in the general case, you need roughly 2n logical (error-corrected) qubits for the algorithm itself, plus substantial overhead for quantum error correction. Realistic estimates for breaking RSA-2048 put the requirement at approximately 4,000 to 10,000 noise-free logical qubits, each requiring anywhere from hundreds to thousands of physical qubits to implement via surface codes.

Where are we? As of early 2026: IBM's Heron processor has ~133 physical qubits with improved error rates. Google's Willow chip demonstrated below-threshold error correction — a genuine milestone. But "noise-free logical qubit" counts remain in single digits to low tens for full error correction. We are roughly three to four orders of magnitude away from breaking RSA-2048.

That sounds like a long way off. And in terms of brute timeline, 10–20 years is a common expert estimate for cryptographically relevant quantum computers. But consider: most RSA-protected data encrypted today could be captured now and decrypted later. This is the harvest now, decrypt later attack — adversaries collect encrypted traffic today, wait for quantum hardware to mature. For long-lived secrets — state intelligence, medical records, financial data — the threat window opens now, not when the first quantum computer arrives.

"The question is not whether cryptographically relevant quantum computers will exist, but when. And for long-lived secrets, 'when' starts affecting your security posture today." — Dustin Moody, NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography Project Lead, 2023

POST-QUANTUM CRYPTOGRAPHY

The response to this threat is already underway. In 2024, NIST finalised its first set of Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) standards — algorithms designed to be secure against both classical and quantum adversaries.

The headline algorithm is CRYSTALS-Kyber (now standardised as ML-KEM), a key encapsulation mechanism based on the hardness of the Module Learning With Errors (MLWE) problem — a lattice-based problem with no known efficient quantum solution. For digital signatures, CRYSTALS-Dilithium (ML-DSA) and SPHINCS+ (SLH-DSA) are the primary standards.

(A full deep-dive into PQC — how lattice cryptography works, why MLWE resists quantum attacks, and the practical migration path — is coming in a separate post. The math deserves its own space.)

The migration question is urgent. TLS 1.3, SSH, certificate authorities, code signing, VPNs, hardware security modules — every layer of the cryptographic stack needs updating. Large organisations are looking at multi-year transitions. The time to start is before quantum computers arrive, not after. NIST has published the standards. The engineering work starts now.

GROVER'S ALGORITHM (BONUS)

Shor's algorithm gets most of the headlines, but there is a second quantum threat that affects a broader range of cryptographic systems: Grover's algorithm.

Where Shor solves a specific mathematical structure (period-finding), Grover's is a general-purpose search speedup. Given an unstructured search space of N items — like searching for a secret key in a space of 2256 possible keys — a classical computer needs O(N) evaluations on average. Grover's algorithm finds the answer in O(√N) steps using quantum amplitude amplification.

Grover's speedup Classical search: O(N) evaluations Grover's search: O(√N) evaluations For AES-256: √(2256) = 2128 quantum evaluations

The practical implication: Grover's algorithm halves the effective bit-security of symmetric-key cryptography. AES-256, which provides 256-bit security against classical adversaries, provides only 128-bit security against a quantum adversary running Grover's. AES-128 drops to 64-bit security — below acceptable thresholds.

The fix is simpler than for RSA: double your key size. AES-256 remains secure in a post-quantum world; AES-128 should be deprecated. Hash functions similarly need output lengths of at least 256 bits (SHA-256 and SHA-384 are fine; SHA-1 and MD5 were already broken for separate reasons). Grover's is a quantitative threat, not an existential one — unlike Shor's, which breaks RSA entirely.

The distinction matters for planning: symmetric encryption can be upgraded by changing a parameter. Public-key infrastructure requires replacing the underlying mathematics. Shor's is the civilisational problem. Grover's is the maintenance item.

A NOTE FROM ME

I first encountered Shor's algorithm in my second year of my MSc, during a module on computational complexity. What struck me then — and still strikes me — is how the algorithm doesn't "attack" RSA by brute force. It finds a trapdoor that RSA's designers didn't know existed, because it only exists in a model of computation they weren't considering.

There is something philosophically unsettling about that. We built an entire global security infrastructure on a mathematical assumption — not a proven theorem, just an observed difficulty. And then someone found a different machine where the difficulty evaporates. The internet's encryption was not broken by a smarter attacker. It was broken by a smarter model of what computation is.

The right response, I think, is not panic — the hardware to run Shor's at scale doesn't exist yet — but not complacency either. The NIST standards are out. The migration tools exist. The threat model is well-understood. The organisations that act now, before quantum hardware scales, will be fine. The ones that wait for a headline will not be.

Post-quantum cryptography is not a distant academic exercise. It is infrastructure work that needs to start this decade. And it is, genuinely, one of the most interesting areas of applied mathematics and computer science right now.
