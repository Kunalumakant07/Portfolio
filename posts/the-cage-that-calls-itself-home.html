THE CAGE
THAT CALLS
ITSELF HOME

How childhood, family, emotion, language, and society install the operating system you will spend your whole life thinking is you. And why the most sophisticated feature of this system is making you feel like you chose all of it.

Umakant Kunal
MSc Computer Science

I want to begin with an observation that I cannot shake: most of the thoughts I think, I did not choose to think. They arise from patterns that were installed in me long before I had the cognitive apparatus to evaluate them — by a family that did not choose its patterns either, by a culture that accumulated its assumptions over centuries, by a language that made certain ideas easy to express and others nearly inexpressible, by a nervous system responding to experiences I had at ages I cannot consciously remember.

And the most disturbing part is not that this happened. It is that the system is so complete, so well-fitted to itself, so resistant to examination from the inside, that the normal experience of a person living within it is not confinement. It is home. The cage does not feel like a cage. It feels like the shape of reality. The walls do not look like walls. They look like the horizon.

I want to pull this apart — from every angle I can find. Philosophy, developmental psychology, sociology, anthropology, neuroscience, Indian thought, the history of science, and the question that sits underneath all of it: is there a way out, and if there is, where does it go?

I — THE INSTALLATION: HOW IT HAPPENS BEFORE YOU CAN SAY NO

The First Years: Writing on Wet Cement

The human brain is born more unfinished than any other animal's. A foal can walk within hours. A human infant cannot do anything except cry and feed for months. This is not a design flaw — it is a design feature. The extended period of helplessness and dependence is precisely what allows the brain to be shaped by its specific environment rather than pre-wired for a generic one. We are the most adaptable species precisely because we arrive so unformed.

In the first three years, the brain forms approximately one million new neural connections per second. This is the critical period — the window during which experience most powerfully shapes the architecture of thought, emotion, and perception. During this window, the child is not yet capable of language-based reflection. They cannot evaluate what is being installed. They can only absorb. Every interaction with a caregiver, every emotional atmosphere in the home, every pattern of attunement or misattunement is being written into neural circuitry at a rate and depth that no later learning can fully equal.

John Bowlby's attachment theory, developed from the 1950s onward, showed that the quality of early attachment to a primary caregiver creates what he called an internal working model — a template for all future relationships, for what intimacy means, for whether other people are fundamentally safe or threatening, for what you must do to receive care. This model is not a belief you hold. It is a perceptual filter you see through. You do not decide, in adult relationships, to expect abandonment or security. You perceive it, because your nervous system learned to perceive it before you had words.

Language: The Invisible Architecture of Thought

At around eighteen months, language acquisition accelerates. And here something extraordinary and alarming happens simultaneously: the child gains immense cognitive power — the ability to represent experience symbolically, to reason, to communicate — and simultaneously inherits a system of pre-existing categories, distinctions, and assumptions that will structure all subsequent thought.

The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis — in its weak form, now broadly accepted — proposes that the language you think in shapes the thoughts available to you. Not that you cannot think beyond your language, but that your language makes certain thoughts easy and others difficult. Russian speakers, with separate basic-level words for light blue and dark blue, perceive colour boundaries more sharply than English speakers. The Hopi language has no grammatical tense — and Hopi speakers, researchers found, conceptualise time differently. The Pirahã people of the Amazon, whose language has no numbers, cannot reliably distinguish quantities above three.

What this means is that the conceptual grid through which you perceive reality — what categories you naturally carve experience into, what distinctions feel obvious and which feel forced — was handed to you by people who lived before you, who themselves inherited it from people who lived before them. The concepts arrived before the experiences they were supposed to describe. You were given a map before you ever saw the territory. And the map is so familiar that you cannot always tell where the map ends and the territory begins.

"The limits of my language mean the limits of my world." — Ludwig Wittgenstein, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, 1921

The Family as the First System

The family does not merely provide food and shelter. It provides the first model of power — who has it, how it is used, whether it is arbitrary or principled, whether it can be questioned. It provides the first model of conflict — whether disagreement is catastrophic or manageable, whether anger destroys or is survived. It provides the first model of love — whether it is conditional or unconditional, whether you must perform to receive it, whether you are wanted for who you are or for what you produce.

None of these models are chosen. None of them are consciously installed. The parents themselves are operating from their own early installations — inherited from their parents, who inherited from theirs. The family is not a collection of individuals making deliberate choices. It is an antenna transmitting the compressed psychological history of its lineage, mostly without knowing it.

Murray Bowen's family systems theory describes how anxiety, dysfunction, and emotional patterns propagate across generations — not through genetics alone, but through the behavioural and relational patterns that shape the developing nervous system. You do not inherit your grandfather's specific trauma. You inherit the emotional atmosphere that his trauma created in your parent, which shaped how your parent related to you, which shaped your nervous system's baseline calibration of safety and threat. The wound does not pass as a story. It passes as a way of being.

// layers of installation — deepest to surface
Nervous system tuning First 0–3 years. Pre-verbal. Attachment patterns, threat calibration, emotional baseline. Cannot be consciously accessed or directly changed.
Language & categories 1.5–7 years. The conceptual grid. What distinctions feel natural, what is nameable, what thoughts are easy or hard. Mostly invisible.
Family models 0–18 years. Power, love, conflict, worth, expectation. Operate as unquestioned assumptions about how relationships work.
Cultural/religious programming Continuous. Shame/pride architecture, moral framework, identity markers, group belonging. Feels like values but was never chosen.
Institutional conditioning School, media, peer groups. The performance of the correct self. Reward/punishment for compliance. Most visible — but the visible layers rest on invisible ones.

II — THE SYSTEM: HOW EMERGENCE LOCKS THE CAGE

Here is the part I find most intellectually fascinating and most personally disturbing. The conditioning I described above does not just shape individual people. It produces a system — an emergent social structure that is self-reproducing, self-correcting, and vastly more stable than any individual human intention within it. Understanding how emergence works here is, I think, crucial to understanding why changing it is so hard.

What Emergence Actually Means

Emergence is what happens when a large number of simple interacting components produce behaviour that could not have been predicted from any individual component. A neuron cannot think. A hundred billion neurons, connected in a particular pattern, produce a mind. A single water molecule is neither wet nor dry — wetness is an emergent property of billions of them interacting. A single human following social norms is not a society. Millions of them, each responding to each other's responses, produce a social structure that has properties — stability, resistance to change, self-regulation — that no individual intended or could have designed.

The critical thing about emergent systems is that they cannot be changed by changing any individual component. You can change one person's beliefs, one family's patterns, one school's curriculum. The system absorbs the deviation, routes around it, and re-stabilises. The system is not controlled by anyone. It does not have a headquarters. It is the pattern that the interactions produce, and it exists at a level above any individual participant.

The System Already Knows What You Will Think

This is the observation I have been sitting with for a long time, and I think it is genuinely important. A sufficiently mature social system does not just respond to deviance. It anticipates it. It has, encoded in its institutions, media, culture, and interpersonal dynamics, pre-prepared responses to every predictable form of resistance.

Question religion? There is a prepared response: your doubt is a spiritual crisis to be healed, not a legitimate inquiry to be answered. Question the education system? The system calls you an idealist, a troublemaker, someone who doesn't understand how the real world works. Question the social order? You are labelled as mentally ill, politically dangerous, or simply immature — someone who will understand when they are older. Every route of questioning has a pre-installed response. The system has been questioned before. It learned. It is not naive.

Pierre Bourdieu called this doxa — the totality of what goes without saying in a given social field. Doxa is not propaganda, which is explicit. Doxa is the layer of assumption so deep and so pervasive that it is never stated, because stating it would require the possibility of its negation, and within the system that possibility does not exist. Fish do not question water. You do not question doxa because doxa is the medium in which questioning itself takes place.

"Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains. One man thinks himself the master of others, but remains more of a slave than they are." — Jean-Jacques Rousseau, The Social Contract, 1762

The Illusion of Growth Within the Cage

The system's most sophisticated feature is the simulation of growth it offers. You study, get credentials, get promoted, travel, form opinions, update your views — and the entire time you are moving through a space that was designed for you to move through. The school prepares you for the university. The university prepares you for the job. The job prepares you for the next job. At each stage there is a metric of success that the system defines and administers. At each stage the feeling of progress is real. The question is: progress toward what? And: who defined the destination?

Ivan Illich, in his 1971 book Deschooling Society, made the uncomfortable argument that institutional schooling does not primarily transmit knowledge. It primarily transmits the belief that knowledge requires institutional transmission — that learning requires school, that healthcare requires hospitals, that politics requires parties. It teaches you to be dependent on institutions precisely by calling this dependence education. The diploma is not evidence that you learned. It is evidence that you were present and compliant in the right sequence of rooms.

John Dewey — a genuine radical in educational philosophy — spent his career arguing for what you instinctively feel: that learning by doing, by direct engagement with problems in the real world, is categorically different from learning by being told. His insight was that genuine learning changes the learner's relationship to the world, not just their inventory of stored information. The system co-opted his language while gutting his meaning — "experiential learning" became a curriculum module, "critical thinking" became an exam skill, "creativity" became a column in the rubric.

The psychologist B.F. Skinner, who spent his career studying operant conditioning in rats and pigeons, made a claim that is still considered radical: human beings are shaped by their reinforcement histories in the same way as any other animal, and the feeling of making free choices is an epiphenomenon of those histories, not a cause of them. Most people dismiss this as too reductive. But the dismissal itself is interesting — it comes with extraordinary emotional force. The question of whether the emotional force is evidence against Skinner's position, or produced by a nervous system that was shaped to protect itself from his position, is not easy to answer from the inside.

III — THE ORIGIN: WHY SOCIETY WAS BORN FROM FEAR, NOT LOVE

You have an intuition I want to take seriously: that society was formed not as a celebration of human connection but as a defensive response to human threat. That people gathered into groups not because they loved each other but because they feared each other — and feared the outside. That the social contract was not a free agreement but a negotiated surrender, each person giving up some freedom in exchange for protection from the freedom of others.

This is Thomas Hobbes' position, stated with cold clarity in Leviathan (1651): in the state of nature, life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." Without the sovereign to enforce peace, every person is at war with every other person — not because humans are uniquely evil, but because in conditions of scarcity and uncertainty, the rational move is to strike first. Society is the structure built to interrupt this dynamic. And the price of interrupting it is submission to authority.

The Anthropological Evidence

The question is whether the Hobbesian picture is historically accurate. For a long time, the dominant anthropological narrative — heavily influenced by Rousseau's "noble savage" — held that pre-agricultural humans lived in relatively egalitarian, peaceful bands and that hierarchy and violence were products of civilisation, not its preconditions. Recent evidence has complicated this considerably.

Research on contemporary hunter-gatherer societies and archaeological analysis of pre-agricultural skeletal remains both show significant levels of interpersonal violence. Lawrence Keeley's 1996 book War Before Civilization analysed archaeological evidence across dozens of pre-state societies and found rates of violent death that dwarf modern state warfare proportionally. The Yanomami people, one of the most studied isolated groups, have a term — waiteri — meaning a combination of ferocity and worth that is the central male virtue. Status and survival are intertwined with the capacity for violence.

But here is where it gets more nuanced. Christopher Boehm's research, particularly in Hierarchy in the Forest (1999), shows that pre-agricultural hunter-gatherer bands were not just naturally egalitarian — they actively and deliberately enforced egalitarianism through what he calls reverse dominance hierarchies. When an individual tried to accumulate disproportionate power, the rest of the group consistently and collectively pushed back — through ridicule, ostracism, and sometimes killing. The egalitarianism was not the absence of power dynamics. It was a continuous collective act of suppression of dominant individuals.

What this suggests is that your intuition is partially right: society as we know it — hierarchical, stratified, with concentrations of power and formal authority — was indeed born partly from fear and the need to manage inter-group violence. But the original human social form was not hierarchical. Hierarchy itself was an invention, and an invention that people resisted for a very long time before it became normalised. The question is why it eventually won. And the answer connects directly back to what we explored in the Animal Farm post: the mechanisms by which power concentrates, perpetuates itself, and comes to feel legitimate.

"For most of history, Anonymous was a woman — or a lower-caste man, or an enslaved person, or a child. The ones history remembered were the exceptions who broke through, not the rule." — paraphrasing Virginia Woolf, A Room of One's Own, 1929

The Samskara: The Indian Framework for Installed Patterns

Sanskrit has a word that I find more precise than anything Western psychology has produced for this phenomenon: samskara (संस्कार). Literally "to put together well" or "to refine" — it refers to the mental impressions, emotional residues, and conditioned patterns of response that are formed through experience and accumulated across a lifetime. Every experience leaves a samskara. Every repeated experience deepens the groove. Every groove makes the corresponding response more automatic — more likely to be triggered by a relevant stimulus without conscious mediation.

The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali, written approximately 400 CE but systematising a far older tradition, describe the mind as a field (chitta) in which samskaras are constantly being formed, strengthened, or weakened. The goal of yogic practice is not to eliminate experience — it is to interrupt the automaticity of the response to experience. To create a gap between stimulus and response. In that gap, choice becomes possible. Without it, you are not choosing — you are executing a programme installed before you had the tools to question it.

This maps precisely onto what neuroscience calls neural plasticity. Repeated patterns of activation strengthen synaptic connections through long-term potentiation. The more a neural pathway is activated, the more efficiently it fires — the more automatic the response becomes. Conversely, patterns that are not activated weaken. The brain is always being written. The question is whether the writing is being done by your present choices or by your accumulated history.

IV — THE RESEARCH QUESTION: WHAT SCIENCE ACTUALLY KNOWS (AND WHAT IT PRETENDS TO)

I want to pause here and say something uncomfortable about the very research I have been citing. Because I believe in genuine science — in the project of trying to understand reality as it is — and precisely because I believe in it, I think it requires honesty about where it is solid and where it is performing solidity it does not have.

The Replication Crisis

In 2015, a large-scale project called the Reproducibility Project attempted to replicate 100 studies from top-tier psychology journals. Fewer than 40% of the original findings held up under replication. Not marginal findings — flagship studies that had been taught in introductory psychology courses for decades, that formed the basis of bestselling books, that had shaped policy and practice.

The ego depletion effect — the idea that willpower is a limited resource that gets "used up" — largely failed to replicate. The power pose effect (Amy Cuddy's famous 2010 study claiming two minutes of expansive posture raised testosterone and lowered cortisol) failed to replicate. The "priming" effects from John Bargh's influential work — the idea that subliminal exposure to words about old age makes people walk slower — largely failed to replicate. Carol Dweck's growth mindset interventions, while more robust than some, have shown much smaller and more conditional effects in large replications than the popular literature suggests.

None of this means psychology is false. It means that a significant proportion of published psychological findings are artefacts of small samples, publication bias (journals publish significant results, not null results), p-hacking (testing multiple hypotheses until one reaches significance), and motivated reasoning in researchers who are human beings embedded in the same systems they are studying. The social pressure to publish, to produce findings that support a theory, to write books and give TED talks — these are not separate from the research. They are inside it.

The Sociological Equivalent

Sociology and anthropology have their own version of this problem. The mid-20th century produced a wave of studies of isolated, pre-contact cultures that were later revealed to be heavily shaped by the researchers' prior assumptions and the power dynamics of fieldwork itself. Margaret Mead's famous studies of Samoan adolescent sexuality — which she used to argue that sexual mores were entirely culturally relative — were challenged by Derek Freeman's counter-research showing she had been misled by her informants. The "peaceful Hopi" and the "violent Yanomami" were both exaggerations produced by researchers looking for examples to fit prior theories.

I am not saying we should distrust all research. I am saying that research emerges from institutions, and those institutions have the same emergent self-reproducing dynamics as all the other systems we have been examining. The sociology of science — how funding, prestige, citation, peer review, and ideological fashion shape which questions get asked and which answers get accepted — is itself a field of study, and one that does not produce comforting results about the objectivity of the enterprise.

The researcher I trust most are those who: publish null results, who actively try to falsify their own theories rather than confirm them, who are transparent about limitations, who are indifferent to whether their findings are popular or convenient, and who — critically — have no financial or reputational stake in a particular outcome. This is Richard Feynman's standard: "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool." Most famous psychology findings violated this standard somewhere in their history.

V — GÖDEL'S GHOST: THE IMPOSSIBILITY OF SEEING THE WHOLE CAGE FROM INSIDE IT

There is a mathematical result that I think has the deepest relevance to this entire question, even though it was proven about formal logical systems, not about human minds or societies. It is Kurt Gödel's Incompleteness Theorem, published in 1931.

Gödel showed that any sufficiently powerful formal system contains true statements that cannot be proven within the system itself. To prove them, you need a larger, more powerful system — which itself will contain unprovable truths, requiring an even larger system, in infinite regress. No sufficiently complex system can fully account for itself from within its own axioms.

I want to use this carefully, as an analogy rather than a direct proof. But the analogy feels right: a mind that is itself a product of a system cannot fully evaluate that system using only the tools the system gave it. The categories with which you question your conditioning were installed by your conditioning. The values by which you judge your society were instilled by your society. The sense of what counts as a legitimate argument, a valid emotion, a rational response — all of it arrived before you arrived. You are trying to audit the accountant with the accountant's own ledgers.

This is not a counsel of despair. Gödel's theorem does not mean formal systems are useless. It means they have inherent limits, and that knowing those limits is more honest than pretending they do not exist. The same applies here. The conditioning is real and deep. You cannot step entirely outside it. But you can become aware of its edges. You can notice the places where your thinking feels automatic rather than chosen, where your emotional reactions precede any thought, where your sense of what is obvious is not shared by people from different systems. These edges are not proof that the cage does not exist — they are the cage, becoming visible.

"We shall not cease from exploration, and the end of all our exploring will be to arrive where we started and know the place for the first time." — T.S. Eliot, Little Gidding, 1942

VI — THE PARADOX OF LEARNING BY DOING IN A SYSTEM THAT REWARDS COMPLIANCE

You believe in learning by doing. By direct experience. By trying things and seeing what happens, rather than being told what should happen and then verifying it. This instinct is epistemically sound — it is, in fact, the fundamental method of science before it was institutionalised. Galileo dropped things and measured them. He did not ask what Aristotle said should happen. The experimental method is learning by doing at a civilisational scale.

But the system does not reward this. The system rewards the correct performance of having-learned. The exam does not test whether you can use knowledge. It tests whether you can retrieve and display it in the expected format. The credential does not verify capability. It verifies compliance with a curriculum. The job interview does not evaluate whether you can do the job. It evaluates whether you can describe yourself in terms that fit the expected description.

Kolb's experiential learning cycle — experience, reflection, conceptualisation, experiment — is a genuine model of how humans actually acquire usable knowledge. It has been cited thousands of times in educational research. It has been incorporated into exactly zero mainstream national examination systems. Because examination systems do not exist to optimise learning. They exist to rank, sort, and certify — to make the allocation of opportunity legible to institutions that need to make decisions about large numbers of people quickly and cheaply.

The tragedy is personal as well as systemic. The person who learns by doing — who acquires deep, practical, integrated knowledge through direct engagement — often cannot demonstrate that knowledge in the forms the system recognises. The credential is not issued for what you know. It is issued for how you performed in the environment where knowing was tested. Genuine understanding frequently looks like hesitation to the system, because genuine understanding includes awareness of complexity. The system rewards the performance of certainty, not its substance.

VII — IS THERE A WAY OUT? AND WHAT DOES "OUT" EVEN MEAN?

I want to be honest here rather than optimistic. I do not think there is an "outside" to get to. The system is not a box you can step out of. It is the medium in which you exist. You cannot breathe outside it because you are made of it. The language you use to question it is the language it gave you. The values that make you want to question it were installed by it. Even this blog post — the act of writing it, the conceptual tools I am using, the form I am expressing it in — is a product of the systems I am interrogating.

But I think there is something real and valuable in the questioning itself, even without a clean destination. And I think it matters for a few specific reasons.

First: awareness changes the relationship, even when it cannot change the fact. You cannot undo your childhood. You cannot unspeaking your mother tongue. You cannot unlearn the emotional patterns that were installed before you had language. But you can become aware of them operating. And awareness — genuine, non-judgemental, accurate awareness — creates a space between the stimulus and the automatic response in which something other than the programme can occur. This is what the Yoga Sutras mean. This is what good psychotherapy aims for. This is what Socratic dialogue tries to produce.

Second: learning by doing is the correct method even within a system that does not reward it. The credential that the system refuses to issue for direct experiential learning is real in its consequences — you may not get the job, the funding, the recognition. That is a genuine cost and I do not minimise it. But the knowledge itself does not care about the credential. The person who has learned through direct engagement, through failure and iteration and genuine contact with reality, has something that the person who performed learning for an exam does not have. The system cannot take that away, even when it refuses to recognise it.

Third: the questioning has a historical function beyond the individual. Every system that has ever changed — every expansion of who counts as a person, who is allowed to speak, who is eligible for rights — happened because some people inside the system were not fully captured by it. Not because they had escaped it. Because they had developed enough distance from some part of it to see it as contingent rather than natural, as constructed rather than given, as something that had been decided by someone at some point rather than as the shape of reality itself. The questioning does not destroy the system. But it creates the possibility of revision that uncritical acceptance forecloses.

"If you can see your path laid out in front of you step by step, you know it's not your path. Your own path you make with every step you take. That's why it's your path." — Joseph Campbell, The Hero's Journey, 1949

VIII — CONNECTING THE THREADS

I want to end by drawing the threads together, including the ones from earlier writing.

In the Animal Farm post, I wrote about how corrupt systems perpetuate themselves through language control, manufactured threat, and the weaponisation of good faith. What I did not fully explore there was the deeper layer: the animals could not overthrow the pigs not only because the pigs were clever, but because the animals had no framework for imagining an alternative. Their language did not contain the concepts. Their emotional patterns were calibrated to submission. Their sense of what was possible had been shaped by the very system they were trying to change. The revolution reproduced the structure of domination because the revolutionaries were made of that structure.

In the alignment post, I wrote about the difficulty of specifying human values because humans cannot agree on what they value. But the deeper problem is what this post has been about: humans often do not know what they value, because what feels like their values are the installed preferences of their conditioning. Aligning AI to human values means aligning it to the output of all the systems I have been describing — the family programming, the cultural framework, the emergent social pressures. Whether that is a good target depends on whether you think those systems, in aggregate, are tracking something genuinely good, or whether they are tracking the preferences of whoever had enough power to shape them. The answer matters enormously.

And in the consciousness post, the question I asked was whether there is anything it is like to be an AI system. The question I want to add now is: is there anything it is really like to be you? Not you as the accumulated residue of everything that was done to you — but you as the awareness that is capable of noticing all of it? The Vedantic tradition has a very clear answer to this. It distinguishes sharply between jiva — the conditioned self, the personality shaped by experience — and atman — the witnessing awareness that is aware of the conditioning without being identical to it. The fascinating claim is that the second one was never installed, never conditioned, and cannot be lost. It is just frequently covered over.

A PERSONAL NOTE

I have lived most of my life feeling that something does not quite fit. That the responses I am supposed to give to the situations I find myself in are not quite mine. That the goals I am supposed to want are someone else's goals that I inherited without being asked. That when I learn something by doing it — by actually touching the problem, breaking it, rebuilding it — I understand it in a way that no curriculum has ever produced in me, and that the curriculum does not recognise.

I do not think this makes me special. I think it makes me someone who has not yet been fully absorbed. The discomfort is the sign of a gap — between the system's expectation and something in me that has not yet conformed to it. That gap is uncomfortable. It is also, I think, worth protecting.

The question I live with is not "how do I escape the system" — I know enough now to know that question has no clean answer. The question is: within the system I cannot fully escape, how do I remain someone who is genuinely trying to see clearly, rather than someone who has made peace with seeing what the system wants me to see?

I do not have a final answer. But I think asking the question, consistently, with as much precision and honesty as I can manage, is the closest thing to freedom that is actually available inside the cage. And that might be enough.

The cage calls itself home. But it is not the only thing you are.
