Conway's Game of Life —
Complexity from Four Simple Rules

In 1970, mathematician John Conway invented a game with no players. Four rules on an infinite grid. No interaction required. And yet from these four rules emerge gliders, spaceships, self-replicators, logic gates, and eventually a universal Turing machine. It remains one of the most profound demonstrations of emergence in science.

Umakant Kunal
MSc Computer Science

In 1970, mathematician John Conway sat down with a pencil, graph paper, and a question: could he design the simplest possible rule set that would produce behaviour no one could predict? He wanted something that didn't converge to a dead grid, didn't explode into chaos, but balanced on the knife-edge between the two. He tried hundreds of rule combinations. He tested them by hand. Then he found it — a cellular automaton so simple it fits on a napkin, and so deep that mathematicians are still discovering new things inside it more than fifty years later. He called it Life.

From four rules on an infinite grid of square cells, Life produces gliders that crawl across the board, guns that fire streams of gliders indefinitely, logic gates built entirely from glider collisions, memory registers, and ultimately — astonishingly — a universal Turing machine. Nothing in the rules mentions any of this. Gliders are not special. Logic gates are not encoded anywhere. They are properties that emerge, unbidden, at a higher level of description. This is what makes Life more than a curiosity: it is a controlled laboratory for studying one of the deepest questions in science. Where does complexity come from?

THE FOUR RULES

The rules of Conway's Game of Life are applied simultaneously to every cell on an infinite two-dimensional grid. Each cell is either alive (on) or dead (off). Each cell has exactly eight neighbours — the cells that touch it horizontally, vertically, and diagonally. Every generation, the following four rules are applied:

  1. Survival: A live cell with exactly 2 or 3 live neighbours survives to the next generation.
  2. Birth: A dead cell with exactly 3 live neighbours becomes alive in the next generation.
  3. Underpopulation: A live cell with fewer than 2 live neighbours dies — as if from loneliness.
  4. Overpopulation: A live cell with more than 3 live neighbours dies — as if from overcrowding.

That is everything. No exceptions. No special cases. No memory of previous states. The entire future of any configuration is determined by these four sentences applied to each cell in parallel.

Why these particular rules? Conway spent years refining them, guided by a set of explicit design criteria. The results should be unpredictable — no simple formula should let you fast-forward to a future state. Growth should be possible but not guaranteed; most patterns should eventually die, stabilise, or oscillate, but a few should grow forever. The rules should balance between explosive growth (too many births, the grid fills completely and dies) and total extinction (too many deaths, everything vanishes). Life sits in the narrow band where interesting things happen — the zone computer scientists now call the edge of chaos. Conway didn't just stumble onto this. He engineered it.

"The Game of Life was specifically designed so that simple initial configurations would produce a range of complex behaviour — stable, periodic, and apparently chaotic — making it almost impossible to predict the fate of a general starting configuration." — John Horton Conway, interviewed in Scientific American, 1970

THE PATTERNS

After Life was published in Martin Gardner's Scientific American column in October 1970, an entire taxonomy of patterns was discovered and catalogued by an obsessive global community of hobbyists, programmers, and mathematicians. They fall into several clean categories.

Still Lifes

Still lifes are configurations that do not change from one generation to the next. Every live cell in a still life has exactly 2 or 3 live neighbours (so it survives), and every dead cell adjacent to the pattern has fewer or more than 3 live neighbours (so it does not turn on). They are stable fixed points of the Life rule. The two simplest and most common are the block and the beehive.

// still life: block (2×2 square)
  . . . . .
  . ■ ■ . .
  . ■ ■ . .
  . . . . .
// still life: beehive (6 cells)
  . . . . . .
  . . ■ ■ . .
  . ■ . . ■ .
  . . ■ ■ . .
  . . . . . .

The block is the simplest possible still life: a 2×2 square. Every live cell has exactly three live neighbours. Every surrounding dead cell has at most two. It will never change, no matter how long you run the simulation. The beehive — six cells arranged in a hexagonal shape — is the second most common still life encountered in practice. Both are astonishingly common end-states when random patterns are allowed to evolve.

Oscillators

Oscillators are patterns that return to their initial configuration after a fixed number of generations — their period. The simplest is the blinker, a period-2 oscillator: three cells in a row collapse to three cells in a column, then back again, forever.

// oscillator: blinker (period 2)
  Generation 1:      Generation 2:
  . . . . .          . . . . .
  . . ■ . .          . . . . .
  . . ■ . .          . ■ ■ ■ .
  . . ■ . .          . . . . .
  . . . . .          . . . . .

The pulsar is a period-3 oscillator of remarkable symmetry — a large, cross-shaped structure with twelve-fold symmetry that cycles through three distinct phases. It was one of the earliest complex patterns discovered and remains one of the most visually striking. Period-3 oscillators are rarer than period-2 and their discovery was a minor triumph for early Life researchers.

Spaceships

Spaceships are the most astonishing class of Life pattern: configurations that translate themselves across the grid. They move. They are not physically moving — the cells themselves don't relocate — but the pattern reappears, shifted in space, after a fixed number of generations. The most famous is the glider, discovered by Richard K. Guy in 1970, just weeks after Life was published.

// spaceship: glider (period 4, moves diagonally)
  Gen 1:    Gen 2:    Gen 3:    Gen 4:    Gen 5:
  . ■ .     . . .     . . .     . . .     . . ■ .
  . . ■     ■ . ■     . . ■     . ■ .     . . . ■
  ■ ■ ■     . ■ ■     ■ . ■     . ■ ■     . ■ ■ ■
            . ■ .     . ■ ■     . ■ .
                      . . .     (shifted one cell diagonally)

The glider is five cells arranged in an L-shape with one extra. Every four generations it returns to its original shape, but displaced one cell diagonally. It travels at c/4 in the diagonal direction — one quarter of the maximum possible speed in Life. The lightweight spaceship (LWSS) moves horizontally at c/2, twice as fast, and is the smallest known orthogonal spaceship. These were not designed. They were found.

The Glider Gun

In October 1970, Conway offered a $50 prize to anyone who could find a pattern that grew without bound. Most mathematicians expected this was impossible — surely all patterns would eventually stabilise. Bill Gosper, then at MIT, proved them wrong within weeks. The Gosper glider gun is a 36-cell pattern that oscillates with period 30, and every 30 generations it emits a new glider. The gliders travel away from the gun indefinitely. The gun itself never changes. The pattern as a whole grows without bound — exactly what Conway had conjectured was possible but unprovable.

Bill Gosper's discovery of the glider gun in November 1970 was not just a $50 prize win. It was the first demonstration that Life could produce unbounded growth, which was the key prerequisite for proving Turing completeness. Without the glider gun, there is no infinite stream of signals. Without an infinite stream of signals, you cannot build a universal computer. The gun unlocked everything that followed.

EMERGENCE

Here is what makes Life philosophically extraordinary: none of the four rules say anything about gliders. The words "glider", "oscillator", "spaceship", "gun" do not appear anywhere in the rule set. There is no rule that says "under these conditions, a self-translating pattern shall form." And yet they form. Reliably. Predictably. Reproducibly. From the same initial conditions, you always get the same glider. This is emergence — the appearance of properties and behaviours at a higher level of description that are not present at the lower level.

The distinction is crucial. Emergence is not magic. The glider is fully explained by the four rules applied cell by cell — there is no additional ingredient. But the description "a five-cell pattern that translates diagonally every four generations" is not available at the level of individual cell updates. It only becomes visible when you zoom out and describe the system at a higher level of abstraction. The glider is a higher-level pattern that is real — it persists, it interacts with other patterns, it can carry information — even though it has no fundamental status in the underlying rules.

This is the same phenomenon I wrote about when examining complex systems more broadly — the way certain descriptions at higher levels of abstraction are indispensable. You could, in principle, track every cell in the Life grid and never use the word "glider." But you would miss something real. The glider is a causally relevant entity. It interacts with other gliders. It can be blocked, reflected, annihilated. Describing the grid in terms of gliders and guns and logic gates gives you predictive and explanatory power that the cell-level description does not. This is precisely the kind of emergence that appears in chemistry, biology, and neuroscience — and Life gives us a toy model clean enough to study it rigorously.

"The move I want to resist is the idea that once you've described the lower level — the cells and the rules — you've said everything there is to say. The glider is real. It is a stable pattern with causal powers. Insisting it 'reduces' to cell updates in a way that makes it less real is not a scientific insight; it's a philosophical prejudice." — Daniel Dennett, Darwin's Dangerous Idea (paraphrased on real patterns)

Life has become one of the canonical model systems for studying emergence precisely because it is so controlled. The rules are completely known. There is no hidden mechanism. There is no measurement problem. Whatever emerges from Life emerged purely from those four rules, and if you cannot predict it from the rules, that unpredictability is a property of the system itself, not a gap in our knowledge. This makes Life a uniquely honest laboratory.

TURING COMPLETENESS

In 2002, Paul Rendell formally proved what the Life community had long suspected: Conway's Game of Life is Turing complete. Any computation that can be performed by a Turing machine — which is to say, any computation that can be performed at all — can be performed in Life. This is not a metaphor. It is a precise mathematical statement.

The construction works as follows. Gliders serve as signal carriers — a stream of gliders represents a binary signal, with presence encoding a 1 and absence encoding a 0. When two glider streams collide, their interaction can be engineered to produce specific outputs depending on the inputs: this is a logic gate. AND gates, OR gates, NOT gates — all have been implemented in Life using carefully choreographed glider collisions. From logic gates, you can build memory: a feedback loop that maintains a signal indefinitely. From logic gates and memory, you can build any digital circuit. From digital circuits, you can build a computer.

In 2009, Adam P. Goucher constructed a full self-replicating pattern in Life — a configuration that reads its own description, constructs a copy of itself, and launches it. In 2010, a working Turing machine was implemented in Life at a scale of hundreds of thousands of cells. In 2018, Nick Gotts and others have verified larger and more efficient implementations. As of today, a working computer — capable of running any program, including itself — has been built inside a Life simulation.

If four rules applied to a grid of dead and alive cells can produce a universal computer, then the rules of physics — which are also rules applied to a substrate — might be doing something analogous. The complexity of brains, ecosystems, civilisations might not require complexity at the fundamental level. It might require only the right rules applied at sufficient scale. Conway's Life is not proof of this. But it is existence proof that the gap between "four simple rules" and "universal computation" is crossable.

The philosophical implications are hard to overstate. We tend to think that complex outputs require complex inputs — that to get a Turing machine, you need to design a Turing machine. Life shows this is wrong. Universal computation can emerge from rules that have no concept of computation in them. The rules know about cell counts. Nothing else.

CELLULAR AUTOMATA GENERALLY

Life is one particular cellular automaton — but the broader category of cellular automata spans an enormous space of rule sets with wildly varying behaviour. The simplest family is the elementary cellular automata defined by Stephen Wolfram: one-dimensional grids of cells, each with two states, each determined by its own state and its two immediate neighbours. There are exactly 256 such rule sets, each named by a number from 0 to 255 (Wolfram's numbering). Their behaviour spans the full range from completely static to completely random, and some land in the middle with intricate, structured complexity.

Rule 110 is the most striking. A one-dimensional CA, one row of cells, a single three-cell neighbourhood rule — and it is Turing complete. Matthew Cook proved this in 1994 (published 2004). Rule 110 is believed to be the simplest known Turing-complete system. Its output — a cascade of triangular structures, gliders, and interactions — is visually complex but arises from a rule set so minimal it can be written as a lookup table with eight entries.

Wolfram spent over a decade studying cellular automata, and in 2002 published A New Kind of Science — a 1,200-page argument that cellular automata and their ilk are not curiosities but the fundamental mechanism underlying all of nature. His thesis: the universe may be a cellular automaton. Complex physical systems — fluid dynamics, quantum mechanics, biological morphogenesis — might be the emergent behaviour of some simple underlying rule applied to some substrate we have not yet identified. Simple rules, applied repeatedly, at enormous scale and speed, give rise to everything.

The book was and remains controversial. Critics argued that Wolfram overstated his case — that showing simple CAs can produce complex output does not mean the universe is a CA. The identification of which CA, the derivation of known physical laws from CA rules, the experimental predictions — none of these were provided. The response in the scientific community was mixed: genuine interest in the ideas, significant scepticism about the claims, and considerable irritation at what many perceived as insufficient engagement with existing work in physics, mathematics, and computer science. The thesis is not settled. It remains an open and fascinating conjecture.

"My main discovery is that there's a much richer universe of possible programs than I ever imagined, and that even extremely simple programs can produce behaviour of great complexity — behaviour of a kind that one might have thought would require much more elaborate underlying programs." — Stephen Wolfram, A New Kind of Science, 2002

THE PHILOSOPHY

What does it mean that four rules can produce universal computation? I keep returning to this question because I think it changes something fundamental about how we should think about the relationship between rules and outcomes, between mechanism and meaning.

Life is deterministic. Given any initial configuration, every future state is completely determined by the four rules. There is no randomness, no hidden variable, no quantum indeterminacy. If you know the initial state perfectly, you know all future states perfectly — in principle. And yet Life exhibits what Wolfram calls computational irreducibility: for most initial configurations, there is no shortcut to knowing the future state. You cannot calculate what the grid looks like at step one million without simulating all one million steps. The future is determined but not computable in advance.

This is subtle and important. Determinism does not imply predictability. A system can be entirely rule-governed — no free will, no magic, no randomness — and still be effectively unpredictable, because prediction requires running the computation and there is no faster way to run it than at full speed. This is why Conway's design criterion of unpredictability was not just an aesthetic preference: it is a mathematical property of computationally irreducible systems. Life is unpredictable not because we lack information, but because unpredictability is baked into the structure of universal computation.

This has implications beyond cellular automata. If the laws of physics are Turing complete — and there are good reasons to think they are at least as powerful — then the universe itself is computationally irreducible. The future is determined. It is also, in a precise sense, unknowable in advance. Not because of chaos or quantum effects, but because computing the future is the same computational problem as running the future. The universe is its own fastest simulation.

There is also the question of what it means to call something a rule. The four rules of Life do not know about logic gates. Does that mean logic gates are not "real" in Life? Most people's intuitions say no — the logic gate is real, it does things, it is a genuine feature of the world of Life. But if logic gates are real even though they are not in the rules, then the question of what is "fundamental" becomes genuinely complicated. In Life, cells are fundamental. Logic gates are derived. But derived does not mean fictitious. Derived means emergent. And emergence, in this sense, is not a failure of reduction — it is the nature of complex systems.

CONWAY THE MAN

John Horton Conway died on 11 April 2020, in Princeton, New Jersey, from complications of COVID-19. He was 82. He had been a professor at Princeton since 1987, and before that at Cambridge, where he had spent most of his career. He died in the first weeks of a global pandemic, in a season of institutional closure and collective isolation, in a way that felt almost like a paradox given the subject of his most famous work — a model of life and death, persistence and extinction, built on a grid of cells.

Conway was one of the most original mathematicians of the twentieth century, and Game of Life was, by his own account, not his most important work — merely his most famous. His real mathematical legacy runs deeper. He invented surreal numbers: a vast generalisation of the real number line that includes infinitesimals and transfinite numbers, discovered by Conway almost as a byproduct of analysing combinatorial games. He made major contributions to the classification of finite simple groups, including the discovery of three new sporadic groups — the Conway groups Co₁, Co₂, and Co₃. He worked on the Monster group, the largest sporadic simple group with approximately 8 × 10⁵³ elements, and contributed to the discovery of Monstrous Moonshine — the astonishing and completely unexpected connection between the Monster group and the j-function in number theory, a result that earned Richard Borcherds a Fields Medal in 1998.

Conway reportedly found Game of Life mildly embarrassing — it occupied too much of his time and overshadowed work he considered more significant. But he also understood its importance. He had found something at the edge of simplicity and complexity that nobody had found before. A game with no players. A universe in a grid. Four rules. Everything else: emergent.

"I'm not particularly proud of the Game of Life. I thought of it as a minor thing. But I'm proud that it had such a big impact. It showed people that very simple rules could produce very complex behaviour. I think that was a surprise to a lot of people." — John Horton Conway, interview with Siobhan Roberts, Genius at Play, 2015

What Life tells me — personally, as someone who spends time thinking about computation, complexity, and what it means to understand a system — is that the gap between rules and reality is not what we think it is. We intuitively feel that simple rules should produce simple outcomes. That to get a Turing machine, you need to build a Turing machine. That consciousness, if it exists, requires some elaborate special mechanism at the level of fundamental physics. Life challenges all of this. Not by proving any of it wrong, but by demonstrating — clearly, unambiguously, in a system where everything is visible and verifiable — that the jump from four rules to universal computation is possible. That emergence is real. That the map from substrate to structure can be nonlinear in ways that surprise even the people who designed the substrate.

I think about that sometimes when I read about neuroscience, or consciousness, or the origins of biological complexity. We have the neurons. We have the wiring rules, approximately. And we are surprised that something called "awareness" or "thought" emerges from firing patterns in meat. But if you had never seen a glider before, you would be surprised by that too. A five-cell pattern that moves. Nothing in the rules says it should. And yet there it is, crossing the grid, carrying information, interacting with the world, stubbornly real.

Conway found something profound in a grid of squares and four rules about counting neighbours. The profundity was not in the rules. It was in what the rules implied — at a higher level of description, across time, at scale. That is, I think, where most of the interesting things in the universe live.
