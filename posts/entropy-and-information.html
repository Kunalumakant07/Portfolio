Entropy, Information,
and Maxwell's Demon

In 1867, a thought experiment exposed the deepest connection in all of science. It took ninety years and the invention of information theory to close the loop. Thermodynamic entropy and Shannon entropy are not analogues — they are the same thing, seen from different angles.

Umakant Kunal
MSc Computer Science

In 1867, James Clerk Maxwell — the same Maxwell who unified electricity and magnetism into a single framework, who wrote down the four equations that Einstein called the most important in physics — invented a demon. Not a theological entity. A thought experiment. A tiny, imaginary creature stationed at a small door between two gas-filled chambers, watching individual molecules zip past and deciding, with perfect intelligence and no apparent effort, which to let through and which to block.

Fast molecules to the right. Slow molecules to the left. One chamber grows hot. The other grows cold. A temperature gradient appears, without burning any fuel, without doing any work. You can now run a heat engine off that gradient — useful energy, apparently from nothing. The second law of thermodynamics, which says that entropy can only ever increase in a closed system, appeared to have been defeated by a sufficiently clever observer.

It took ninety years to explain why the demon cannot work. The resolution is not in the demon's muscles, not in the friction of the door, not in any mechanical inefficiency. The resolution requires information theory — a field that did not yet exist when Maxwell wrote the letter to his colleague Peter Guthrie Tait describing the thought experiment. And when the explanation finally came, it revealed something extraordinary: the two most important entropy concepts in all of science — one from physics, one from communication theory — are the same concept in disguise.

THERMODYNAMIC ENTROPY

To understand what Maxwell's demon was attacking, you need to understand what entropy actually measures. The thermodynamic entropy of a system is not, at its core, a measure of mess or disorder in any loose, intuitive sense. It is a precise count of possibilities.

In 1877, Ludwig Boltzmann wrote a single equation on his tombstone — or rather, the equation that would later be inscribed there by admirers who understood its importance. The formula is deceptively simple:

S = kB ln W S is entropy (joules per kelvin)  ·  kB is Boltzmann's constant (1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K)
W is the number of distinct microstates consistent with the observed macrostate

W — from the German Wahrscheinlichkeit, probability — is the number of ways you can arrange the microscopic components of a system while producing the same macroscopic appearance. Consider a box of gas. You can measure its temperature, pressure, and volume. But underneath that macroscopic description, trillions of molecules are bouncing around in an astronomically large number of specific arrangements, each producing those same bulk properties. That count is W. The logarithm ensures entropy is additive across independent systems. The Boltzmann constant converts from abstract counts into the physical units we measure with thermometers.

Why does entropy always increase? Not because some force drives systems toward disorder. Because disordered states are overwhelmingly more numerous than ordered ones. If you take a box of gas carefully arranged so all fast molecules are on the right and all slow molecules are on the left, and you let it evolve, it will mix — not because mixing is somehow mandatory, but because there are fantastically more mixed configurations than sorted ones. The system wanders randomly through its possible states, and the random walk almost certainly leads away from rare, special arrangements toward the vast majority of configurations, which happen to look uniform and mixed.

This is the arrow of time. The past is the direction of lower entropy; the future is the direction of higher entropy — not because the laws of physics distinguish them (they are almost entirely time-symmetric at the microscale), but because we started in an unusually low-entropy state and have been moving toward the overwhelmingly more probable high-entropy configurations ever since. The universe is climbing a probability slope. The second law is statistics at cosmic scale.

MAXWELL'S DEMON

Now Maxwell's demon makes precise sense — and its apparent power becomes clear. The demon watches individual molecules. It has knowledge the macroscopic observer does not: it knows the velocity of each molecule, not just the average. Armed with this information, it can sort: open the door for a fast molecule heading left, close it for a slow one. No force is applied; the door is frictionless; the demon expends no energy in the classical sense. Yet the result is a decrease in entropy. The mixed gas becomes sorted.

The system has moved from a high-W state (many configurations consistent with uniform temperature) to a low-W state (far fewer configurations consistent with the sorted arrangement). Entropy has decreased. The second law is violated. The demon has created order — and therefore, via a heat engine, useful work — from nothing but knowledge.

"If we conceive of a being whose faculties are so sharpened that he can follow every molecule in its course, such a being, whose attributes are still as essentially finite as our own, would be able to do what is at present impossible to us. For we have seen that the molecules in a vessel full of air at uniform temperature are moving with velocities by no means uniform... Now let us suppose that such a vessel is divided into two portions... and that this being, who I shall call for conciseness a demon, watches for the swifter molecules and opens the hole when one comes and lets it pass..." — James Clerk Maxwell, letter to P. G. Tait, 1867

For ninety years, physicists tried to kill the demon by finding a hidden cost. Maybe the door has friction. Maybe measuring the molecules requires light, and that light pressure does work. Leo Szilárd in 1929 came closest to the right answer: he showed that a one-bit measurement — which side is the molecule on? — is associated with an entropy cost of kB ln 2. But Szilárd located the cost in the measurement itself, which turned out to be wrong. Measurement, as Charles Bennett would later show, can in principle be done reversibly and for free. The real cost is elsewhere.

The demon remained alive, killing the second law, for nearly a century.

SHANNON ENTROPY

In 1948, Claude Shannon — a mathematician at Bell Labs who had spent the war years working on cryptography and signal processing — published "A Mathematical Theory of Communication." It is one of the most consequential papers of the twentieth century. Shannon was not thinking about thermodynamics. He was thinking about telegrams. He wanted to know: how much information is in a message?

His answer was a formula that bears a striking resemblance to Boltzmann's entropy — so striking that when Shannon asked John von Neumann what to call it, von Neumann reportedly said: "Call it entropy. In the first place, a mathematical development very similar to yours already exists in Boltzmann's statistical mechanics. In the second place, and more importantly, nobody knows what entropy really is, so in a debate you will always have the advantage."

H = −Σ pi log2 pi H is information entropy (bits)  ·  pi is the probability of the i-th symbol
Binary entropy: H(p) = −p log2(p) − (1−p) log2(1−p)

Shannon's H measures uncertainty. More precisely, it measures the average surprise per symbol in a message. A fair coin flip — heads or tails with equal probability — has maximum binary entropy: H = 1 bit. You know nothing before the flip; you learn one full bit after it. A biased coin that lands heads 99% of the time has low entropy: before the flip, you're already almost certain of the outcome. The coin tells you almost nothing new. A message composed entirely of a single repeated character has zero entropy — no uncertainty, no information content, no surprise.

Shannon's fundamental theorem — the source coding theorem — states that the maximum lossless compression rate for a data source is its entropy. You cannot compress a source below its entropy without losing information. A random sequence, by definition of maximum entropy, is incompressible. The entropy is the irreducible information content; everything above it is redundancy that can be stripped away.

"The fundamental problem of communication is that of reproducing at one point either exactly or approximately a message selected at another point. Frequently the messages have meaning; that is they refer to or are correlated according to some system with certain physical or conceptual entities. These semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem." — Claude Shannon, A Mathematical Theory of Communication, 1948

The resemblance between H and S is not cosmetic. Shannon's formula is Boltzmann's formula with kB = 1 and the units changed from energy to bits. They describe the same underlying mathematical structure: a measure of the number of possibilities consistent with what you know. Boltzmann counts the microstates consistent with a macrostate. Shannon counts the messages consistent with a probability distribution. They are the same count, expressed at different scales.

LANDAUER'S PRINCIPLE

In 1961, Rolf Landauer — an IBM physicist — published a short paper that changed everything. Landauer asked a deceptively simple question: what is the minimum thermodynamic cost of a computation? Not the cost of transistors switching, not the cost of current flowing, but the absolute minimum cost, the floor below which no physical implementation can go.

His answer: most operations are reversible and have no minimum cost. But erasing a bit of information costs at least kB T ln 2 of heat, dissipated irreversibly into the environment. No physical process can erase information without paying this price. It is not an engineering limitation; it is a fundamental law.

"Information is physical. Any computation requires a physical substrate to carry it, and the laws of physics constrain what computations are possible. Logically irreversible operations — such as erasure — must dissipate energy. This is not a limitation of current technology. It is a law of nature." — Rolf Landauer, IBM Journal of Research and Development, 1961

Why does erasure cost energy? Because erasing a bit is thermodynamically irreversible. Before erasure, the bit is in one of two states — 0 or 1 — and the environment doesn't know which. After erasure, the bit is in a known state — say, 0 — and the environment does know. One bit of uncertainty has been moved from the memory into the environment as thermal noise. By the Boltzmann formula, reducing W by a factor of 2 decreases entropy by kB ln 2. By the second law, this decrease in entropy must be compensated by an increase of at least kB ln 2 elsewhere — specifically, as heat dissipated into the thermal environment. The physical cost of forgetting is exactly kB T ln 2 per bit, where T is the temperature of the environment.

At room temperature (T ≈ 300 K), the Landauer limit is approximately 2.9 × 10⁻²¹ joules per bit erased — roughly 10 million times smaller than the energy a modern transistor dissipates per operation. We are nowhere near the fundamental limit. But as computing scales down, it matters enormously. Biological neurons may already operate near it.

Landauer's principle is the bridge. It translates between Shannon's information entropy — a count of bits — and Boltzmann's thermodynamic entropy — a count of physical microstates. Every bit of information that a physical system stores corresponds to a thermodynamic entropy of kB ln 2. Information is not abstract. It has weight, in exactly the sense that it has a thermodynamic cost. Information is physical.

MAXWELL'S DEMON RESOLVED

In 1982, Charles Bennett completed the resolution that Landauer had begun. Bennett showed that Szilárd had been wrong about where the cost lay. Measurement is reversible — the demon can watch molecules and record what it sees without dissipating energy. The demon can sort molecules, open and close the door, accumulate a temperature gradient, extract useful work — all without violating the second law. So far.

The catch is memory. The demon must remember what it observed in order to decide whether to open the door. Every measurement fills a register. After N molecule-sorting operations, the demon holds N bits of information about which molecules went where. Its memory fills up.

To continue operating, the demon must reset its memory — erase those N bits to free up space for the next round of observations. And here is where Landauer's principle strikes. Erasing N bits of memory costs N × kB T ln 2 of heat, dissipated into the environment. That heat is exactly equal to the work the demon extracted from creating the temperature gradient. The books balance. The second law survives.

"The demon has to forget what it has learned. Forgetting is not free. The thermodynamic cost of erasing the demon's memory is precisely equal to the thermodynamic benefit of sorting the molecules. The second law wins — but it wins through information theory, not through any mechanical argument." — Charles Bennett, The Thermodynamics of Computation, 1982

The resolution teaches something deeper than a bookkeeping fix. It establishes that computation has irreducible physical costs — not because of engineering imperfection, but because of the laws of thermodynamics applied to information. A reversible computation costs nothing in principle. But any computation that discards intermediate results — which every practical computation does — pays the Landauer price. The universe charges for forgetting. Memory is not free. Intelligence is not free.

The demon failed because knowledge accumulates. It cannot watch molecules forever without filling its memory, and it cannot empty its memory without paying exactly the thermodynamic cost it thought it was avoiding. The second law is not defeated by intelligence — it applies to intelligence. Any observer, any computer, any mind must pay thermodynamic costs to process information. Thinking has a price denominated in entropy.

THE HOLOGRAPHIC PRINCIPLE REVISITED

If information is physical, then the amount of information a region of space can hold must be limited by physics. This is not a storage engineering problem. It is a consequence of general relativity and quantum mechanics combined.

Jacob Bekenstein showed in the early 1970s that the entropy of a black hole — and therefore the maximum information it can store — is proportional to the area of its event horizon, not its volume. The Bekenstein-Hawking entropy formula (which I explored in detail in my earlier post on black holes and information) gives:

SBH = kB · A / (4 · ℓp²)

where A is the horizon area and ℓp is the Planck length. This is startling. Our intuition says storage scales with volume — more space, more bits. But for a black hole, maximum entropy scales with surface area. And since a black hole is the densest object physics permits, it sets the upper bound for any system. You cannot store more than one bit per four Planck areas of boundary surface. The universe's information capacity is a property of surfaces, not volumes.

This is the holographic principle, and it connects directly to Shannon entropy. Each Planck-scale cell on the event horizon encodes one bit — exactly Shannon's bit, the fundamental unit of uncertainty. The Bekenstein-Hawking formula is Boltzmann's formula applied to the quantum geometry of spacetime. The same mathematical structure — entropy as the logarithm of the number of possible states — appears at every scale from coin flips to cosmology.

The implication is profound. If the information content of any region is bounded by its surface area, then perhaps the three-dimensional world we inhabit is in some sense a projection from a two-dimensional boundary. Juan Maldacena's AdS/CFT correspondence — the most precisely tested conjecture in modern theoretical physics — makes this concrete: a gravitational theory in a volume is exactly equivalent to a quantum field theory on its boundary. The universe may be running on a lower-dimensional substrate than it appears. Shannon entropy and black hole thermodynamics are not merely analogous; they may be the same entity viewed through different mathematical lenses.

ENTROPY IN MACHINE LEARNING

The connection between entropy and learning is not metaphorical. It is the literal mathematics underlying most of how we train machine learning systems today.

When you train a classifier with cross-entropy loss, you are directly minimising Shannon entropy between two probability distributions. If the model predicts distribution Q over classes and the true distribution is P, the cross-entropy loss is:

H(P, Q) = −Σ P(x) log Q(x)

This decomposes as H(P) + DKL(P ‖ Q): the entropy of the true distribution (irreducible) plus the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the two. Training minimises the KL divergence — it moves the model's predictions closer to the truth in the information-theoretic sense. Cross-entropy loss is literally the number of bits wasted because you used Q to encode a message that was actually drawn from P.

In maximum entropy models, we go the other direction: given known constraints, choose the distribution that maximises entropy — that makes the fewest additional assumptions, that is maximally uncertain about what isn't known. This principle underlies softmax, logistic regression, and the foundations of Bayesian reasoning. The model that minimises unjustified certainty is the model that best represents what the data actually says.

The connection to compression — and therefore to Kolmogorov complexity, explored in my previous post — is tight. A model that has learned the true distribution of a data source can compress that source to its Shannon entropy. Learning and compression are dual operations. The best model of a dataset is the shortest program that generates it. Shannon sets the compression floor; Kolmogorov measures the structural complexity beneath it.

THE PHILOSOPHICAL MEANING

Entropy is usually framed as a story of decay. The universe winds down. Useful energy dissipates. Structures dissolve. The second law sounds like a death sentence written into physics. But that reading misses what is actually going on.

Entropy is the arrow of time. The reason the past feels different from the future, the reason causes precede effects, the reason you remember what happened but not what will happen — all of this follows from entropy increase. The second law is not a tax on existence; it is the mechanism by which time has a direction at all. Without entropy, the universe would be time-symmetric and causality would lose its grip.

More profoundly: entropy is the cost of knowledge. Maxwell's demon showed that an observer who knows more than the thermodynamic limit permits will pay the excess as heat. Landauer made this quantitative. Intelligence is not exempt from thermodynamics; it operates within thermodynamics, bound by the same accounting. Any mind — biological, artificial, or hypothetical — that processes information must dissipate energy. The physical cost of one thought, one decision, one bit of memory erased, is kB T ln 2. Consciousness, if it processes information, generates entropy. Thinking heats the universe.

This has led some physicists to propose that information is not merely described by physics but is its substrate. Physicist John Archibald Wheeler spent the last decades of his career developing what he called "it from bit": the idea that every physical quantity, every field, every particle, derives its existence from information — from yes-or-no questions, from binary distinctions. Reality is not made of matter or energy in the first instance; it is made of information, and matter and energy are what information looks like when it is embodied.

"It from bit. Otherwise put, every it — every particle, every field of force, even the spacetime continuum itself — derives its existence, its meaning, its very existence entirely from apparatus-elicited answers to yes-or-no questions, binary choices, bits. It from bit symbolises the idea that every item of the physical world has at bottom — a very deep bottom, in most instances — an immaterial source and explanation; that which we call reality arises in the last analysis from the posing of yes-or-no questions." — John Archibald Wheeler, A Journey into Gravity and Spacetime, 1990

Erik Verlinde has proposed that gravity itself is an entropic force — not a fundamental interaction but an emergent consequence of the tendency of information on holographic surfaces to increase entropy. Max Tegmark argues that the universe is not merely described by mathematics but is a mathematical structure, with physical existence and informational structure being identical. These are speculative ideas, contested and unresolved. But they share a common thread: information is not a human construct imposed on nature. It is nature.

This matters for artificial intelligence in a way that is easy to overlook. We build systems that process information at scale — billions of parameters, petabytes of training data, inference at the cost of megawatts. Each forward pass, each gradient update, each weight erasure during training pays the Landauer price. We are not running abstract mathematical operations; we are performing thermodynamic processes. The intelligence we construct, like all intelligence, generates heat. The limits of computation are not engineering limits. They are physics.

A PERSONAL NOTE

I came to this intersection from both directions, which might be why it feels like home. On one side: physics textbooks, thermodynamics problem sets, entropy as a quantity you compute for Carnot cycles. On the other side: information theory, compression algorithms, cross-entropy loss functions, the strange way that a neural network training curve and a thermodynamic cooling curve look structurally similar if you squint.

The moment the connection clicked — really clicked, not just as an intellectual acknowledgement that the formulae look alike — was reading Bennett's 1982 paper on the thermodynamics of computation. The argument is so clean. The demon fails not because it lacks power, not because the door has friction, but because it cannot forget what it knows without paying for it. Memory is entropy. Forgetting is heat. The second law is not broken by intelligence; it governs intelligence.

Standing at the intersection of physics, information, and computation, I find myself asking questions that feel genuinely open. What is the thermodynamic cost of the model I'm training? What does it mean that the same mathematical object — entropy — describes the disorder of a gas, the uncertainty in a message, the information density of a black hole, and the loss function of a neural network? Is this a coincidence of formalism, or is it telling us something deep about the structure of reality?

I think it is the latter. I think Maxwell's demon, still haunting physics more than 150 years after its invention, is pointing at something fundamental: that intelligence, information, and physics are not three separate domains but one domain, seen at different scales. The demon was never going to win. It was playing a game whose rules are written in entropy — and entropy always collects.
