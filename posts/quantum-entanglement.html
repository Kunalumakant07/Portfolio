Quantum Entanglement —
Einstein's 'Spooky Action'
Finally Explained

Einstein spent the last thirty years of his life convinced quantum mechanics was incomplete. He coined the phrase spooky action at a distance as a term of mockery. Experiments have since proven he was wrong — and what's actually true is far stranger than his discomfort could have imagined.

Umakant Kunal
MSc Computer Science

In a letter to his close friend Max Born written in March 1947, Albert Einstein expressed something that had been gnawing at him for over a decade. He had looked at the equations of quantum mechanics — equations he had helped build, whose predictions were verified to breathtaking precision — and he found them philosophically intolerable. Specifically, he found intolerable the idea that measuring a particle in one location could instantaneously affect the state of another particle arbitrarily far away. He called it spukhafte Fernwirkung — spooky action at a distance — and he meant it as a reductio ad absurdum. Surely, he felt, no serious theory of nature could contain such a thing.

Einstein was not being capricious. He had spent his career insisting that physics must be local — that what happens here cannot be caused by something happening simultaneously over there, with no physical signal travelling between them. His special relativity was built on this. And he believed that physics must be realistic — that physical quantities have definite values whether or not anyone is measuring them. Quantum mechanics, in his reading, violated both principles. He was certain it was incomplete, a useful calculational tool pointing at a deeper, more sensible theory waiting to be discovered.

He spent the last three decades of his life looking for that deeper theory. He never found it. More importantly, the experiments that have been run since his death have demonstrated with extraordinary confidence that he was wrong — not because quantum mechanics is incomplete, but because nature itself violates the intuitions Einstein was defending. The universe is genuinely, irreducibly non-local in a precise sense that we now understand rather well. Entanglement is not a flaw in the theory. It is a feature of reality.

WHAT ENTANGLEMENT ACTUALLY IS

To understand entanglement, you first have to understand superposition. A quantum system — say, a single electron — does not have a definite spin until it is measured. Before measurement, it exists in a superposition: a weighted combination of all the possible outcomes. When you measure it, the superposition collapses to one definite value. The probability of each outcome is determined by the coefficients of the superposition, and the whole business is described by the Schrödinger equation with extraordinary mathematical precision.

Now take two particles. In general, you would describe the state of two particles by combining their individual states: particle A is in some superposition, particle B is in some superposition, and the combined state is the product of those two. These particles are said to be separable. But quantum mechanics allows — and physics routinely produces — two-particle states that cannot be written as any such product. These are entangled states.

The simplest example is a Bell state, also called an EPR pair. Consider two qubits — quantum bits, each capable of being 0 or 1 — prepared in the following joint state:

|Φ⁺⟩ = (1/√2)( |00⟩ + |11⟩ ) Bell State Φ⁺ — maximally entangled two-qubit state

Read this carefully. This state says: there is a 50% probability of finding both qubits in state 0, and a 50% probability of finding both qubits in state 1. There is zero probability of finding them in different states. But crucially — and this is the part that disturbed Einstein — before you measure, neither qubit has a definite state at all. The joint system is in superposition. The individual particles do not have individual states that you simply do not know yet. The quantum state is genuinely and completely described only at the level of the pair.

When you measure qubit A and find it is 0, qubit B is instantly in state 0 as well — with certainty. Not because any signal travelled from A to B. Not because B was secretly 0 all along and you just didn't know it. But because the joint state of the system has collapsed, and B's state is now determined by the entanglement relation that existed before any measurement was made. This works regardless of the distance separating A and B. The two particles could be on opposite sides of the galaxy.

THE EPR PARADOX

In 1935, twelve years before that letter to Born, Einstein had formalised his discomfort into an explicit argument. Together with Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen, he published a paper titled Can Quantum-Mechanical Description of Physical Reality Be Considered Complete? The paper presented what we now call the EPR paradox.

Their argument was careful and logical. They started from two premises that seemed, to any reasonable physicist, non-negotiable. First: locality — physical processes in one region of space cannot instantaneously affect physical processes in a distant, isolated region. Second: realism — if you can predict with certainty the value of a physical quantity without disturbing the system, then that quantity must have a definite value, an element of physical reality, independently of measurement.

They then considered two entangled particles separated by a large distance. By measuring particle A, you can predict with certainty what you will get if you measure particle B — without in any way disturbing B (because the particles are far apart). By their realism criterion, B must already have had a definite value. But quantum mechanics says it didn't. Therefore, the argued, quantum mechanics is incomplete. There must be hidden variables — additional information, not captured by the quantum state, that determines the outcomes of measurements in advance.

"I cannot seriously believe in the quantum theory because it cannot be reconciled with the idea that physics should represent a reality in time and space, free from spooky actions at a distance." — Albert Einstein, letter to Max Born, 1947

The EPR paper was enormously influential. For nearly three decades, the question of hidden variables was treated as a matter of philosophical taste — a debate about interpretation with no experimental consequences. Most working physicists adopted the Copenhagen interpretation and got on with calculating. Bohr published a lengthy reply to EPR arguing, in his typically difficult prose, that the EPR premises themselves were muddled. But nobody thought the question was experimentally decidable.

"There is no quantum world. There is only an abstract quantum physical description. It is wrong to think that the task of physics is to find out how nature is. Physics concerns what we can say about nature." — Niels Bohr

They were wrong about the unresolvability. In 1964, a physicist named John Bell quietly changed everything.

BELL'S THEOREM

John Stewart Bell was a particle physicist at CERN who was deeply troubled by the foundations of quantum mechanics in a way most of his colleagues were not. He took the EPR argument seriously and asked a precise mathematical question: if hidden variables do exist — if there is additional information pre-determining measurement outcomes — what constraints does that impose on the statistics of measurements?

Bell proved in 1964 that any theory of hidden variables satisfying local realism — meaning, roughly, that the hidden variables are locally carried by each particle and cannot be influenced faster than light — must produce measurement correlations that satisfy certain mathematical inequalities, now called Bell inequalities. The key insight: quantum mechanics predicts correlations that violate these inequalities. The two theories make different, testable, numerical predictions. This transformed a philosophical debate into a physics experiment.

To understand Bell's setup, imagine you can measure the spin of each entangled particle along any axis you choose. You and a collaborator each independently choose a measurement direction, measure your respective particles, and record the results. If you do this many times across many different pairs and many different angle choices, you build up a statistical picture of the correlations. Bell showed that for any local hidden variable theory, the correlations measured at different angles must satisfy an inequality of the form:

|E(a,b) − E(a,c)| ≤ 1 + E(b,c)

where E(a,b) is the correlation between measurements along directions a and b. This is the CHSH form of Bell's inequality. Quantum mechanics predicts that for the right choice of angles, the left side can reach 2√2 ≈ 2.828, while the right side is at most 2. The violation is not marginal. It is substantial and unambiguous.

"It is the de Broglie–Bohm theory and its extensions which seem to me to show most clearly that the wave function of the world does not collapse, but that some other description of the quantum world must be sought." — John S. Bell, Speakable and Unspeakable in Quantum Mechanics, 1987

In 1982, French physicist Alain Aspect and his team at Paris-Saclay performed the first truly convincing experimental test of Bell's inequalities. They measured polarisation correlations between entangled photon pairs across a series of different angle settings, closing the major loopholes that had plagued earlier attempts. The result was decisive: nature violates Bell's inequalities. The violation matched the quantum mechanical prediction to high precision. The experiments have been repeated many times since, with ever-tighter loophole closure — most definitively in the 2015 "loophole-free" Bell tests in Delft, Vienna, and NIST that eliminated simultaneously the detection loophole and the locality loophole.

What this means is not subtle. It means that no theory of local hidden variables can reproduce the predictions of quantum mechanics. Einstein's programme — the search for a deeper, local, realistic theory — was not merely unfulfilled. It was shown to be impossible. The universe does not work that way. Entanglement is real, non-local, and has no classical explanation.

WHAT IT DOESN'T ALLOW

At this point a reasonable person would ask: if measuring particle A in London instantly determines the state of particle B in Tokyo, can we use this to send information faster than light? The answer is no — and understanding why is one of the more instructive exercises in quantum reasoning.

The correlation is real. When Alice measures her qubit and gets 0, Bob's qubit is now definitely 0. The problem is that Alice cannot control what result she gets. Her measurement outcome is random. When she measures, she gets 0 with 50% probability and 1 with 50% probability — and she has no way to force the outcome. From Bob's perspective, before he hears anything from Alice, his qubit behaves exactly as if it were a random bit. He gets 0 half the time and 1 half the time. He cannot tell, from his measurements alone, whether Alice has measured yet or not.

The correlation only becomes visible when Alice and Bob classically compare their results — calling each other on the phone, for instance. Only then does the pattern emerge: whenever Alice got 0, Bob also got 0; whenever Alice got 1, Bob also got 1. But that classical comparison is limited by the speed of light. The quantum correlation exists at a distance, but it carries no signal because neither party controls the shared random outcome.

This principle — that entanglement cannot be used for superluminal signalling — is called the no-communication theorem, and it is one of the firmest results in quantum information theory. The non-locality of entanglement is a statistical, relational fact. It does not manifest in any single measurement; it only emerges in the comparison. Nature manages to be non-local without permitting faster-than-light causation. It is an extraordinarily tight constraint, and it is satisfied exactly.

WHAT IT DOES ALLOW

Entanglement is useless for sending information, but it is enormously useful for other things — things that have no classical equivalent.

Quantum Teleportation

In 1993, Bennett, Brassard, Crépeau, Jozsa, Peres, and Wootters showed that entanglement allows quantum teleportation: the transfer of an arbitrary quantum state from one location to another, using a pre-shared entangled pair plus two classical bits of communication. Alice has a qubit in an unknown state that she wants to send to Bob. They share an entangled pair. Alice performs a joint measurement on her unknown qubit and her half of the entangled pair, obtaining two classical bits. She sends those bits to Bob over an ordinary channel. Bob applies a correction operation to his half of the entangled pair, and his qubit is now in exactly the state Alice's qubit was in.

Note carefully what has not happened: no matter has been transported. Alice's original qubit is destroyed in the measurement — this is why teleportation does not violate the no-cloning theorem. What has been transferred is the quantum state, the information content. The two classical bits are necessary, which is why teleportation does not violate no-signalling either. Teleportation has been experimentally demonstrated across increasing distances, and it is a foundational primitive for quantum networking.

Superdense Coding

The flip side of teleportation: entanglement allows Alice to communicate two classical bits of information to Bob by sending only one qubit. This is superdense coding. Starting from a shared entangled pair, Alice applies one of four possible unitary operations to her qubit — each encoding a different two-bit message. She sends the qubit to Bob. Bob performs a joint measurement on both qubits and recovers the two classical bits. Without the pre-shared entanglement, one qubit can carry at most one classical bit. Entanglement effectively doubles the classical information capacity of a quantum channel.

Quantum Key Distribution (E91)

Artur Ekert's 1991 E91 protocol uses entangled pairs for cryptographic key distribution with security guaranteed by the laws of physics rather than computational hardness. Alice and Bob each measure their respective qubits from a stream of entangled pairs at randomly chosen angles. They publicly announce their measurement bases and discard the results from incompatible measurements. The remaining results are correlated in a way that allows them to distill a shared secret key. Any eavesdropper intercepting the particles would disturb the entanglement, causing detectable anomalies in the Bell inequality statistics. Security is not assumed — it is tested on every run. E91 was a landmark in quantum cryptography and directly foreshadowed the quantum network protocols being deployed today.

THE PHILOSOPHICAL FALLOUT

Bell's theorem and the experiments that followed it do not just close a debate about hidden variables. They force a confrontation with something deep about the structure of reality. The theorem shows that any theory that reproduces quantum mechanics must give up either locality (no faster-than-light influences) or realism (physical quantities have definite values independent of measurement) or both. You cannot keep both and save the appearances.

Most physicists, if pressed, will say they sacrifice realism. The lesson is that quantum particles do not carry definite properties until measurement — and this is not a statement about our ignorance, but about the structure of the world. The universe is not a collection of objects with fixed properties being observed; it is a network of relations, potentialities, and correlations that crystallise into definite facts only through interaction. This is not interpretation. It is what the mathematics says, confirmed by the experiments.

Others sacrifice locality, pointing out that the non-local correlations of entanglement, while they cannot carry signals, do seem to constitute a genuine dependence between spacelike-separated events. The de Broglie-Bohm pilot wave interpretation takes this route: it is explicitly non-local and deterministic, reproducing all of quantum mechanics by having a universal wave function that guides particle trajectories at-a-distance. Bell himself was attracted to this approach, precisely because it made the non-locality explicit rather than concealing it.

The measurement problem lurks beneath all of this. When exactly does a quantum state collapse? Schrödinger's cat is not merely a joke; it is a real question about where the quantum world ends and the classical world begins. Eugene Wigner sharpened this into what is now called Wigner's friend: if a conscious observer inside a lab makes a measurement, collapsing the wavefunction from their perspective, but a second observer outside the lab treats the entire lab — observer included — as a quantum system still in superposition, who is right? Recent experiments extending the Wigner's friend scenario have shown that different observers can disagree about facts in ways that have no classical analogue. The measurement problem is not solved. It may be the deepest open question in the foundations of physics.

"Anyone who is not shocked by quantum theory has not understood it." — Niels Bohr

Bohr said this with equanimity, almost pride. The shocking nature of quantum mechanics was, for him, the mark of a genuine theory of nature rather than a projection of classical intuitions onto the microworld. Whether you find that comforting or maddening probably says something about your temperament. I find it both.

There is a particular experience I keep returning to when I think about entanglement. It happens at the moment when you understand the mathematics well enough to follow every step — you can write down the Bell states, trace through the correlation calculations, reproduce the inequality derivation — and you then sit back and try to visualise what is actually happening. And you cannot. Not because you lack the knowledge. But because there is nothing to visualise. The reality that quantum mechanics describes is not a picture you can form. It is a set of equations, confirmed by experiments, that describe correlations without any underlying mechanism you could draw.

This is the peculiar condition of working in quantum information: you hold a theory that is mathematically exact and philosophically incomprehensible at the same time. The formalism works. The predictions are right. The technology is being built. And yet if someone asks you what is really happening when two entangled particles are measured, the honest answer is: we do not know. Not in the sense of lacking data. In the sense that the question may not have the kind of answer we instinctively reach for.

Einstein wanted physics to describe a reality that was local, real, and sensible. He was entitled to that wish. The universe declined to oblige. What we have instead is something stranger and, in its way, more interesting: a theory that works without a picture, a world that is correlated without communication, and a discipline — quantum information science — that has learned to build useful things out of the strangeness rather than waiting for the discomfort to resolve. I find that extraordinary. I find it unsettling in exactly the right way.
