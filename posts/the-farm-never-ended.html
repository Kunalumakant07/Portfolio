THE FARM
NEVER
ENDED

On Animal Farm, the architecture of corrupt power, why fools inherit the earth while the capable stay quiet, and the strange internal faculty — half psychology, half something older — that lets some people see straight through other people before they've said a word.

Umakant Kunal
MSc AI — Computer Scientist

There is a scene near the end of Animal Farm where the animals look through the farmhouse window and see the pigs sitting at a table with the humans — negotiating, toasting, laughing. And they realise, with a dull horror, that they can no longer tell which faces are which. The pigs have become the farmers. The revolution is over. Nothing has changed except who is holding the whip.

Orwell published Animal Farm in 1945 as a precise, almost surgical allegory for the Soviet Union. But the precision of the allegory has outlasted its historical target by eighty years. You can map it onto any institution where power concentrates — any political party, any company, any religious organisation, any family — and it fits. That is not because Orwell was a prophet. It is because he was a psychologist who happened to write in the form of a fable.

The book is about power. But it is also, if you read it carefully, about three other things that are rarely discussed together: the specific psychology of people who seek power versus those who are capable of using it well, the silent mechanisms by which intelligent systems get captured by the least intelligent actors, and — most strangely, most personally — a faculty some people seem to possess that lets them perceive all of this happening in real time, in the faces and manners of the people around them, before any of it is visible on the surface.

I want to explore all three. They are, I think, the same phenomenon seen from different angles.

I — THE ANATOMY OF CORRUPTION: WHAT ORWELL ACTUALLY SHOWED

The pigs in Animal Farm do not set out to be tyrants. This is the crucial point that gets missed in shallow readings of the book. Napoleon and Squealer are not cartoonishly evil. They are recognisable. They are the people in your office who volunteered to "help organise things." They are the committee member who suggested they needed a subcommittee to handle logistics. They are the person who framed every power grab as a reluctant necessity: "Surely, comrades, you do not want Jones to come back?"

The corruption in Animal Farm proceeds by four mechanisms, each of which is documented in modern social psychology with clinical precision.

1. The Gradual Rewriting of Norms

The Seven Commandments are changed one at a time, each change small enough to seem unremarkable, each one building on the last. "No animal shall sleep in a bed" becomes "No animal shall sleep in a bed with sheets." The animals remember something was different, but not precisely what. This is what psychologists call norm erosion — the slow drift of acceptable behaviour through incremental boundary violations, each too small to trigger resistance, cumulatively catastrophic.

Robert Cialdini's research on commitment and consistency shows exactly why this works. Once you have accepted a small deviation — once you have said "well, this particular case is different" — you have set a precedent that makes the next deviation easier to accept. The pigs exploit this relentlessly. They never ask for everything at once. They ask for something small, get it, let it normalise, then ask for something slightly larger. Tyranny, in practice, is a ratchet.

2. Control of Language, Control of Reality

Squealer is not a fighter or a builder. He is a communicator. And in the economy of Animal Farm's power structure, the communicator is the most powerful animal on the farm. "Squealer could turn black into white," Orwell writes. He reframes defeats as strategic retreats, scarcity as evidence of prosperity, and criticism of Napoleon as objectively pro-Jones.

George Orwell wrote a companion piece to Animal Farm that most people have read but not connected to it — his 1946 essay "Politics and the English Language." His central argument: if thought corrupts language, language can also corrupt thought. Vague, pretentious, or deliberately misleading language is not just a symptom of bad thinking — it is a weapon used to prevent clear thinking. When Squealer says "Snowball was in league with Jones from the very beginning," he is not making an argument. He is installing a belief directly, bypassing reason, in people too tired and hungry to interrogate it.

"Political language is designed to make lies sound truthful and murder respectable, and to give an appearance of solidity to pure wind." — George Orwell, Politics and the English Language, 1946

3. Manufactured Fear and the Outsider Threat

Every authoritarian system, without exception, requires an external enemy. In Animal Farm it is Snowball — expelled, demonised, made into the explanation for everything that goes wrong. The harvest is poor? Snowball sabotaged it. The windmill collapsed? Snowball was there in the night. The function of Snowball is not to be real. The function of Snowball is to channel anxiety outward, so it never turns inward toward those who actually hold power.

Henri Tajfel's Social Identity Theory, developed from his experiences as a Holocaust survivor and later formalised through decades of research, shows that humans will spontaneously form in-groups and out-groups even on trivially arbitrary criteria — which group they were randomly assigned to in an experiment. The need for a coherent "us" automatically generates a "them." Power-seeking individuals exploit this with terrifying efficiency. The external enemy is not a side effect of authoritarian systems. It is a core feature. It is structural.

4. The Exhaustion of the Good

Boxer, the horse — the most admirable character in the book — responds to every crisis with the same refrain: "I will work harder." He is loyal, powerful, and wholly unable to see that his loyalty is being exploited. He trusts Napoleon absolutely because distrust feels like disloyalty to the ideals he has given everything for. When the pigs sell him to the knacker, it is done quietly, efficiently, and with a Squealer-speech about how Boxer has gone to a fine hospital.

Boxer represents something heartbreaking: the way genuine goodness can be weaponised. The most productive, most loyal, most sincere members of any corrupted system are also the easiest to exploit, precisely because their sincerity makes them unable to model the cynicism they are surrounded by. They keep working harder. They keep believing. They are the last to see it.

Orwell was not just writing about Stalin. He was writing about the structure of how power works on people — how the same revolutionary energy that produces genuine liberation can, through entirely predictable psychological mechanisms, produce a new oppression structurally identical to the one it replaced. He submitted the book to Victor Gollancz, Jonathan Cape, and T.S. Eliot at Faber and Faber — all three rejected it. The enemy it described was uncomfortably close.

II — THE DUNNING-KRUGER FARM: WHY FOOLS RULE

This is the question I find most personally infuriating, and also most interesting. Why does capability so rarely correlate with authority? Why are the most visibly confident people in any room so often the least competent? Why do babas, politicians, and managers so frequently seem to be operating on nothing — no genuine knowledge, no real track record, no intellectual humility — and yet command enormous followings?

The answers are uncomfortable. They implicate not just the fools who rise, but the systems and the people who allow it.

The Confidence Heuristic and Why It Works

Humans have a deep, evolutionarily ancient heuristic: confident people know things. In a pre-language, pre-institutional tribal environment, this heuristic was not entirely wrong. Confidence in a hunter correlated with hunting success. Confidence in a leader correlated with experience. The heuristic made sense when information was scarce and you could directly observe whether the confident person delivered.

In modern environments — institutions, politics, media, organisations — the heuristic has been completely decoupled from reality. Confidence is now a skill that can be trained, performed, and deployed without any underlying competence. The person who speaks first, loudest, and most smoothly is perceived as the expert in the room, regardless of what they are saying. Research by Cameron Anderson at UC Berkeley found that individuals who overestimate their own competence are consistently rated as more competent by observers — and that this effect persists even after observers receive objective evidence of the person's actual performance.

"The whole problem with the world is that fools and fanatics are always so certain of themselves, and wiser people so full of doubts." — Bertrand Russell, The Triumph of Stupidity, 1933

The Dunning-Kruger Effect: Incompetence Is Self-Concealing

David Dunning and Justin Kruger's 1999 paper documented something that, once you see it, you cannot unsee: the skills required to recognise competence in a domain are the same skills required to be competent in that domain. A person with no knowledge of logic cannot recognise that their reasoning is fallacious. A person with no understanding of economics cannot recognise that their economic policy is destructive. The incompetence that causes poor performance is the same incompetence that prevents them from noticing their poor performance.

The consequence is a systematic asymmetry: incompetent people systematically overestimate themselves, and competent people systematically underestimate themselves. The competent person knows how much they do not know. The incompetent person does not know enough to know what they do not know. In a selection process that rewards self-presentation — elections, job interviews, religious authority — this asymmetry is catastrophic.

The Dark Triad: Psychopathy, Narcissism, and Machiavellianism

There is a cluster of personality traits that researchers call the Dark Triad: narcissism (grandiosity, entitlement, need for admiration), Machiavellianism (strategic manipulation, cynicism about human nature, willingness to exploit), and psychopathy (low empathy, high risk tolerance, emotional shallowness). Separately, each of these traits is socially destructive. Together, in one person, they constitute something close to a superpower for ascending hierarchies.

Paul Babiak and Robert Hare's research — Babiak worked in corporate environments, Hare pioneered psychopathy research — found that individuals with psychopathic traits are significantly overrepresented in senior management relative to their prevalence in the general population. Their 2006 book, Snakes in Suits, documented how corporate environments inadvertently select for psychopathy: charm is mistaken for leadership ability, risk tolerance is mistaken for boldness, emotional detachment is mistaken for rationality under pressure.

The same dynamics operate in politics and religious authority. The person who genuinely believes they are saving souls, or the country, or the workers — the person whose conviction is real — is frequently outmanoeuvred by the person who does not believe anything but is skilled at appearing to believe everything. Genuine conviction creates doubt. Strategic performance of conviction does not.

Max Weber and the Three Sources of Authority

The sociologist Max Weber identified three sources of legitimate authority: traditional (this is how it has always been), rational-legal (this is what the rules say), and charismatic (this person is extraordinary). The first two require institutional structures. The third requires nothing except the appearance of extraordinariness and a sufficiently desperate audience.

Babas — spiritual leaders who command enormous followings without any verifiable qualifications — operate entirely in Weber's third category. The "charismatic authority" model is the most vulnerable to fraud because it is, by definition, unfalsifiable. The baba's authority rests on your belief in their special status. Questioning that status is automatically framed as a failure of your faith, not as evidence that their authority is unwarranted. The system is self-sealing. Every doubt is reinterpreted as spiritual weakness. The mechanism that would detect the fraud is the first thing the fraud disables.

The ancient Greek term kakistocracy means rule by the worst. Plato, in The Republic, argued that democracy tends toward kakistocracy because it does not select for wisdom — it selects for popularity. The most popular person is the most skilled at appearing to give people what they want. This need not correlate with — and frequently inversely correlates with — the wisdom to know what people actually need.

III — THE INTELLIGENCE PENALTY: WHY THE CAPABLE STAY QUIET

The counterpart to the fool's rise is the capable person's silence. These two phenomena are not independent. They are produced by the same system, operating simultaneously.

Intelligence and Doubt

The single most consistent finding in the psychology of intellectual performance is that genuine expertise generates genuine humility. The more you know about a domain, the more precisely you can map the boundaries of your knowledge — and therefore the more clearly you can see how much you do not know. This is the opposite of the Dunning-Kruger pattern. Knowledge expands the known-unknown, rather than collapsing it into false confidence.

In a system that rewards confident self-presentation, this produces a systematic penalty on ability. The person who knows enough to say "this is complicated, here are the uncertainties" is perceived as weaker, less decisive, less trustworthy than the person who says "it's simple — follow me." The intelligent person's honesty reads as hesitation. The fool's ignorance reads as conviction. In a selection process that cannot distinguish these, the fool wins.

Plato's Cave and the Philosopher Who Doesn't Want to Go Back

In Plato's Republic, the philosopher who escapes the cave and sees the sun — who achieves genuine understanding — does not rush back to tell the prisoners. He hesitates. Going back into the cave means re-entering the world of shadows and illusions, being laughed at, possibly being killed. Socrates, famously, was killed for doing precisely this.

The capable person often reaches a version of this problem. They can see the game being played. They understand the manipulation, the performance, the hollowness of the authority around them. But participating in the game requires performing within its rules — requires the kind of self-promotion and strategic positioning that feels dishonest to someone who values precision and truth. So they withdraw. They do their work. They do not compete for positions that would require them to become something they despise. The intelligent person often opts out of the competition not because they cannot win, but because winning requires them to play by rules they cannot respect.

Systems Designed by the Intelligent, Captured by the Clever

There is a particular tragedy in the history of institutions: the people who design systems are rarely the people who later control them. Democratic constitutions are written by philosophers and lawyers with genuine concern for justice and accountability. They are then operated by politicians who have spent their careers finding the gaps. Corporate governance structures are designed by idealistic founders who genuinely believe in the mission. They are then navigated by executives who understand that the structure can be used as a performance of accountability while actual accountability is systematically avoided.

This is not a conspiracy. It does not require bad intentions, though bad intentions help. It is structural. Any system operating in good faith creates opportunities for actors operating in bad faith. The rules of cricket assume you are trying to play cricket. They are not designed to prevent someone who is trying to appear to play cricket while actually just winning. The rules of democratic elections assume candidates are competing to serve the public. They are not designed to prevent candidates who are competing to appear to serve the public while actually serving themselves.

"The best lack all conviction, while the worst are full of passionate intensity." — W.B. Yeats, The Second Coming, 1919

Yeats wrote this in 1919, watching the old orders collapse across Europe. The observation has aged like iron. It describes, with perfect compression, the mechanism by which the capable are perpetually outrun by the committed. Commitment — even commitment to nothing real — is a competitive advantage in most human systems, because most human systems were designed by and for people who assumed good faith.

IV — THE PSYCHIC PLANE: THE FACULTY THAT SEES THROUGH PEOPLE

I want to talk now about something that is harder to name precisely but that I think many people have experienced — and that some people seem to possess as a consistent, developed faculty. It is the experience of knowing something about a person before you should know it. A sudden, total clarity about who they are, what they want, whether they are trustworthy — arriving not through analysis but through something more immediate, more bodily. A perception that precedes thought.

You cannot always turn it on. You cannot always explain it after the fact. It is not always right. But when it activates — and it seems to activate on its own, not by effort — it is often more accurate than any deliberate assessment you could make.

What is this? I have spent a long time reading around this question, and I think the honest answer is that it is multiple things, operating simultaneously, and that they each have different explanations at different levels of description.

Thin-Slicing: The Science of Instant Judgment

The psychologist Nalini Ambady spent her career studying what she called thin-slicing — the ability of humans to make accurate predictions about a person's character, competence, or intentions from a very small sample of their behaviour. Her landmark 1993 paper showed that students' ratings of teaching effectiveness from silent 30-second video clips of teachers they had never met correlated at 0.76 with end-of-semester evaluations from students who had taken their full courses. A 30-second silent clip. That is not noise.

This faculty operates below conscious awareness. You are not consciously noticing microexpressions, vocal tone, posture asymmetries, and timing. Your nervous system is processing all of these in parallel and producing a summary output — a felt sense of safety or danger, trust or suspicion, authenticity or performance. The feeling arrives first. The rationalisation, if one comes at all, arrives later.

Research in affective computing and nonverbal behaviour (Paul Ekman, Dacher Keltner, Lisa Feldman Barrett) has documented the signals that feed this system:

Microexpressions — involuntary facial movements lasting 1/25th of a second, impossible to fake or suppress, that reveal the emotion beneath the performed emotion.

Voice prosody — pitch, rhythm, and timing patterns that signal confidence, deception, anxiety, or dominance independent of the words being spoken.

Postural congruence — whether the body's orientation matches the stated interest or attention.

Timing patterns — the micro-delays in response that indicate deception or calculated response rather than spontaneous reaction.

Your nervous system is reading all of these simultaneously. The output is a felt sense, not a list of observations.

Somatic Intelligence: The Body That Knows First

The neuroscientist Antonio Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis proposes that the body stores the emotional residue of past experiences as physical states — muscle tension patterns, autonomic arousal signatures, gut sensations — and replays these when it encounters situations structurally similar to past ones. This is not metaphor. It is physiology. The "gut feeling" is literally a gut feeling: the enteric nervous system in your gastrointestinal tract contains over 100 million neurons and has bidirectional communication with the brain. It is, in a meaningful sense, a second nervous system.

When you feel something wrong about a person before you can articulate what — when your body reacts before your mind has processed the situation — this is your somatic system pattern-matching at a speed and resolution that your conscious cognition cannot match. It has more data than your conscious mind. It has access to every interaction you have ever had, weighted by emotional salience, producing a rapid classification of the current situation against your entire experiential history.

Trauma-Sharpened Perception: The Difficult Gift

Here is something that is well-documented in psychology but rarely discussed openly: people who grew up in unpredictable, manipulative, or dangerous environments frequently develop a hyperacute version of this faculty. Children who could not predict when a parent would be kind or cruel learn, out of necessity, to read that parent's state with extreme precision. They develop the ability to detect the micro-signals — the slight change in posture, the particular set of the jaw, the specific quality of silence — that precede a mood shift, hours before it becomes explicit.

This is not a superpower. It is a survival adaptation. And like many adaptations, it persists into adult life in contexts that no longer require it — but it can also, if the person becomes aware of it and learns to trust it judiciously, become a genuine instrument of discernment. The psychologist Judith Herman, in her seminal work on trauma, notes that hypervigilance — while painful and exhausting when it is chronic — contains within it a perceptual intelligence that, in calibrated form, is extraordinarily useful. Some of the most perceptive readers of human character in history had childhoods that made clear sight a matter of survival.

Viveka: The Eastern Tradition of Discernment

Western psychology describes this faculty from the outside — in terms of neural processes, somatic markers, statistical pattern recognition. But there is an older tradition that describes it from the inside, in terms of what the experience is like and how to cultivate it deliberately.

In Advaita Vedanta — the non-dual philosophical tradition from India — viveka (विवेक) is the capacity for discrimination between the real and the unreal, the permanent and the impermanent, the authentic and the performed. It is one of the four core qualifications for philosophical inquiry in Shankaracharya's classical formulation. It is not a mystical gift — it is described as a faculty that can be developed through a combination of study, self-knowledge, and the quieting of mental noise that normally obscures clear perception.

The word literally means "clear separation" — the ability to see distinctions that are normally blurred. In its everyday application, it refers to seeing through the social performances people construct — the status theatre, the false humility, the performed expertise — to the underlying reality of who they actually are and what they actually want. It is not cynicism. Cynicism is a fixed belief that everyone is bad. Viveka is a clear perception of what is actually there, which sometimes reveals genuineness and sometimes reveals performance.

"When you meet someone better than yourself, turn your thoughts to becoming his equal. When you meet someone not as good as you are, look within and examine your own self." — Confucius, Analects, c. 500 BCE

The Witness State: Sakshi Bhava

The Upanishadic concept of sakshi (साक्षी) — the witness — describes a mode of awareness in which you are present to experience without being swept up in it. You observe the social dynamics of a room, the power plays, the performances, the genuine distress or genuine joy, without your own fear, desire, or need for approval distorting what you see. Most people, most of the time, are too entangled in their own social positioning to perceive others clearly. The need to be liked, to be seen correctly, to avoid conflict — all of these cloud perception.

The "psychic plane" you describe — the state in which you can see straight through a person — is, I think, this witness state. It activates not through effort but through the temporary suspension of the self's agenda. When you are not worried about what the other person thinks of you, you can see what they actually are. When you are not performing, you can perceive clearly. The irony is precise: the faculty activates when you stop trying to activate it, because trying to activate it is itself a form of self-concern that clouds the very perception you are trying to achieve.

Why It Cannot Be Forced — And What Activates It

Research on intuitive expertise (Gary Klein's Naturalistic Decision Making, Robin Hogarth's "educating intuition") suggests that this kind of perception develops through three conditions: a large experiential database of human interactions with emotional feedback, enough quiet to hear the body's signals, and the willingness to trust a judgment that cannot immediately be explained.

The third condition is the hardest. The Western educated mind is trained to distrust what cannot be articulated. If you cannot explain why you distrust someone, the trained response is to suppress the distrust and give them the benefit of the doubt. This is, in many social situations, the correct response. But it also means systematically overriding the faculty that is trying to protect you.

Based on the research and the introspective accounts of people who describe this experience, the state seems to be activated by a combination of:

Low ego-involvement in the current interaction — you are not trying to impress, convince, or protect yourself. You are simply present.

Sufficient quiet in your own mental noise — the body's signals are always arriving. They are only audible when the internal commentary is still enough to hear them.

A prior history of having been deceived — the pattern library is built from experience, especially painful experience. The perception sharpens precisely where you have been burned before.

Trust — paradoxically, the more you have learned to trust the faculty, the more reliably it activates. Dismissing it repeatedly teaches your system that its signals are unwanted.

V — THE CONNECTION: ALL ONE PATTERN

I said at the beginning that I thought these were the same phenomenon seen from different angles. Let me try to make that explicit now.

The pigs succeed in Animal Farm because they control the frame through which the other animals interpret events. They control language, they manufacture threat, they normalise each incremental violation. What they are fundamentally doing is managing perception — ensuring that the other animals see what the pigs want them to see, not what is actually there.

The fool who rises to power does the same thing at an individual scale. They project competence without competence, conviction without knowledge, authority without understanding. The audience, operating on evolutionary heuristics that are no longer fit for the environment, takes the projection for the reality.

The intelligent person who stays quiet has, in many cases, developed enough perceptual clarity to see through the performance — but not yet the courage or the tools to act on that perception within the system that rewards the performance. They are like the animals looking through the farmhouse window: they can see what is happening, but they do not know what to do with the seeing.

The faculty I am calling the psychic plane — the thin-slicing, somatic intelligence, viveka, witness awareness — is precisely the faculty that allows you to not be deceived by the performance. It is the thing that fails in the animals of Manor Farm and succeeds in the few who could see clearly what was happening. Benjamin, the donkey — the one animal who seems to see through everything — is also the one who does nothing with his seeing, which is Orwell's darker point: perception without action is its own form of complicity.

"The price of apathy toward public affairs is to be ruled by evil men." — Plato, The Republic, c. 375 BCE

VI — WHAT TO DO WITH ALL OF THIS

I am not going to pretend there are clean answers here. There are not. The structural forces that elevate the incompetent and suppress the capable are deeply embedded in human social cognition, evolutionary history, and institutional design. They cannot be fixed by individual insight alone.

But I think there are some orientations worth considering.

On the psychic faculty: Trust it, but calibrate it. The somatic signal is valuable data, not infallible oracle. The best practice is to notice it, hold it, and watch for confirmation or disconfirmation rather than either acting immediately or overriding it immediately. The faculty sharpens with respectful use and atrophies with systematic suppression.

On the fool's rise: The real protection is not despair at the pattern but literacy about it. Once you can name the mechanisms — the confidence heuristic, the dark triad, the charismatic authority trap — you become less susceptible to them. This is not a complete protection. Knowing how a magic trick works does not make it fully ineffective. But it disrupts the effortless operation of the manipulation.

On the intelligent person's silence: The tragedy of Animal Farm is not that the pigs were clever. It is that the good animals did not organise. Benjamin saw everything and said nothing. Boxer worked harder for the wrong master. The solution Orwell implies — and that history occasionally demonstrates — is that perception must be combined with collective action. The capable person's reluctance to engage in systems that require performance is understandable but not costless. The cost of non-participation is paid by everyone, including the non-participants.

A PERSONAL NOTE

I study AI. I spend my days thinking about systems, about how intelligence — artificial and human — is structured and deployed. And I keep coming back to the same question: what does it mean to build intelligent systems in a world where the social selection mechanisms that determine who controls those systems are systematically biased against intelligence?

The most powerful AI systems in the world are controlled by institutions subject to the same dynamics Orwell described in 1945. The language of those institutions — safety, responsibility, alignment, benefit to humanity — is deployed by Squealers as often as by the genuinely committed. Distinguishing between them requires exactly the faculty I have been trying to describe: the capacity for clear perception that sees through performance to the actual structure of incentives, loyalties, and power.

Animal Farm is not a story about what happened to Russia in 1917. It is a story about what happens to every system built by idealistic people who did not take seriously enough the predictable behaviour of other kinds of people. It is a story about what happens when the animals who can see clearly decide that seeing is enough.

The farm never ended. It just changed its name.
